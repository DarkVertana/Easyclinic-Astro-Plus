A clinic rarely finds pilferage as a single event. The count of an antibiotic is a few strips short one month and a box of injections is missing the next, and each shortfall is written off as an adjustment. By the end of the year the write-offs look like the cost of running a pharmacy, and nobody can say how much of it walked out of the door.

This post is for the owner of a clinic with its own dispensary and for the manager of a chain with a pharmacy at every branch. It sets out the controls that make every stock movement traceable, and a one-month check you can run on one category before you change anything else.

## Where pilferage hides

Pilferage sits on the books beside losses that have honest causes:

- A batch that expired on the shelf and was written off at the stocktake.
- A delivery that came in short of the invoice and was booked in without a count.
- Medicines dispensed to a patient and never billed, or billed as a cheaper item.
- A sale or a return entered late, or not at all.

Each of these leaves the same gap between the stock book and the shelf. Until a clinic can tell them apart, every shortfall becomes an adjustment, and an adjustment with no reason is where theft hides. The controls below put a name and a reason against every movement, so the gap that is left is small enough to ask about.

Start with the items that are costly and fit in a pocket. They are where a small loss each week adds up.

## Tie every stock movement to one person

The first control is accountability by name. Every delivery booked in, every medicine dispensed, every transfer and every adjustment is made under the login of the person who did it. A shared login at the pharmacy counter defeats this, because a shortfall then belongs to "the pharmacy" and to nobody in it.

The second half is tying what leaves the shelf to a patient. When a medicine is dispensed against a prescription and goes on a bill, the stock count has a patient and a doctor behind it. A medicine that leaves with neither is the exception to look for.

Tell the staff why before you start. Named movements protect an honest pharmacist, because a shortfall can be traced to a shift instead of being blamed on whoever is on duty when it is found.

In Easy Clinic, the prescription the doctor writes in the EMR reaches the pharmacy screen, and what the pharmacist dispenses goes on the patient's bill and comes off the stock count. Staff roles decide what each person can do in the inventory.

## Approve every adjustment

An adjustment is the one entry that changes the count without a patient or a supplier behind it, so it is where a loss is easiest to hide. Two rules keep it honest. The person who counts the shelf is not the person who approves the adjustment. And every adjustment carries a reason in words: expired, damaged, short on delivery or not found.

"Not found" is the reason to watch. A branch that records a few every month is telling you something, and so is a branch that never records one.

Read the list of adjustments once a week, by item and by the person who asked for them. The same item going missing on the same shift is a pattern worth a conversation. A single shortfall usually is not.

In Easy Clinic, adjustments for shortfalls, excess stock, expired products and damaged products need authorisation and leave an audit log. You can add approval steps with more than one level where you want them.

## Dispense the batch nearest its expiry first

Expiry losses and theft get mistaken for each other in both directions. A box that expired at the back of the shelf can look like a theft at the stocktake, and a theft can be written off as expiry if nobody checks which batch it was.

The World Health Organization's [good storage and distribution practices for medical products](https://www.who.int/publications/m/item/trs-1025-annex-7) say stock should follow the first expired, first out (FEFO) principle: the stock with the earliest expiry date is used before the same item with a later date. That means the batch nearest its date goes out first, whichever batch arrived first. First in, first out works only when every delivery expires later than the one before it, and deliveries do not always arrive that way.

Track expiry by batch. An expiry write-off then names the batch and its date, and it can be checked against the delivery that brought it in. A write-off marked as expired that names no batch is a question to ask.

In Easy Clinic each batch is tracked with its expiry date. The counter is warned at the time of sale when a batch is near its date, and the batch closest to its date is put forward to dispense first. The module also has expiry reports.

## Count the costly items every week

An annual stocktake finds a gap months after it opened, when nobody remembers the week it happened. A cycle count finds it while they still do. Count the costly and most-taken items every week and the rest on a monthly rotation, so every item is counted several times a year instead of once.

Two habits make the counts worth doing. Count without looking at the figure the system expects, so the count is not nudged towards it. And count before the day's dispensing starts or after it ends, so nothing moves while you count.

In Easy Clinic a stock count can be quick or full, and it can be repeated before any difference is finalised.

## Run a one-month check on one category

Before changing everything, measure one category. It gives you a starting figure, and it shows the staff what the controls are for.

1. Choose one category, such as the injectables or the ten costliest items on your shelf.
2. Count it on the first day of the month and write the count down.
3. Through the month, count every delivery in against its invoice, and make sure everything dispensed goes on a bill.
4. Count again on the last day. The expected stock is the opening count plus what was received, minus what was dispensed and minus what expired or was damaged. The difference between that figure and the shelf is the unexplained loss.

Then compare what was dispensed with the prescriptions and counter sales billed for those items. If the pharmacy dispensed more than the bills account for, the gap is at the counter. If the shelf is short of what the records say is there, the gap is behind it.

## In a chain, watch the transfers

A chain adds one more place for stock to disappear: between branches. A transfer sent from one branch and never booked in at the other is a loss with two people who can each say it was the other's.

Record every transfer at both ends, and chase any transfer that has been sent but not received by the end of the day. Compare the same item's unexplained losses across branches. When one branch loses far more of an item than branches with a similar number of patients, that branch gets the next unannounced count.

On Easy Clinic's Enterprise plan, stock moves between branches on a transfer that takes it off one branch's count and puts it on the other's. The transfer is tracked from the moment it leaves one store until the other books it in, with an audit log for each step. ## Signs to act now

- Write-offs are rising while patient numbers hold steady.
- The same items are short at every count.
- Arguments with suppliers about what was delivered keep coming up.
- Adjustments are entered with no reason, or always with the same one.

## What the controls cannot catch

Stock that never enters the system leaves nothing to count against. Free samples that are not booked in, and deliveries received without a count, can go missing without a gap on any report. Book everything in at the door.

Two people who approve each other's adjustments can defeat any approval rule. The person who approves should work outside the pharmacy, and in a chain the weekly list should go to someone outside the branch.

Easy Clinic does not run fully offline today, so plan for the day the pharmacy counter loses its connection. Keep a paper record of what is dispensed until the line is back and enter it the same day, because a late entry is where a count goes wrong. A backup connection, such as mobile data on a spare phone, keeps the counter on the system.
