A patient judges a clinic by the doctor and by everyone else they meet: the person on the phone, the receptionist, the nurse and whoever takes the payment. Training is how a clinic makes those meetings go the same way on a busy Monday as on a quiet Thursday, and how a new receptionist learns the clinic's way of doing things instead of inventing their own.

This post sets out what each role needs and how to build a plan from your own records, with a section on training staff when the clinic changes its software.

## What each role needs to learn

### Clinical staff

Clinical training covers the work where a mistake reaches the patient:

- Infection control and hand hygiene
- Using and cleaning the clinic's equipment
- What to do in an emergency, including who calls for help and where the emergency kit is kept
- Collecting, labelling and sending lab samples
- Separating bio-medical waste into the right bags and containers

In India the last item is a legal duty. The [Bio-Medical Waste Management Rules, 2016](https://cpcb.nic.in/uploads/Projects/Bio-Medical-Waste/Bio-medical_Waste_Management_Rules_2016.pdf) count a clinic as an "occupier", and rule 4(g) requires the occupier to train all health care workers and others who handle bio-medical waste at induction and at least once every year, and to report the training in its annual report. Check with your State Pollution Control Board for any later amendment.

Training also has a limit set by the ethics rules. Regulation 1.6 of India's [2002 ethics regulations](https://www.nmc.org.in/page/rules-regulations-rules-regulations-of-erstwhile-mci-code-of-medical-ethics-regulations-2002), which the National Medical Commission [adopted in August 2023](https://www.nmc.org.in/storage/cms/rules-regulation-nmc/KEpwkMzgUwYNR49OrJaqvLcIpeytGPg7XGcorhTg.pdf), says a physician shall not permit an attendant who is neither registered nor enlisted under the Medical Acts to attend, treat or operate on patients where professional discretion or skill is required. Train assistants well for the tasks their role allows, and keep the rest with registered staff.

### The front desk and billing

The desk and billing staff need to be quick and consistent at:

- Registering a new patient and finding a returning one
- Booking, moving and cancelling appointments, and fitting in walk-ins
- Checking a patient's insurance cover and preparing claims, where the clinic takes insurance
- Raising bills and closing the day's cash

Train each role on the screens it uses. On EasyClinic, the reception login that comes with the price is used to register patients, book appointments, raise bills and take payments, and it cannot open the EMR or issue prescriptions. Each person logs in as themselves, so training a receptionist on the doctor's login teaches them screens they will never see.

### Talking to patients

Everyone who meets patients needs practice at the hard moments: an angry patient at the counter, a bill higher than the patient expected, a diagnosis to explain in plain words, and a complaint that may end up as a review. Practise the words in pairs at a staff meeting, not for the first time in front of a queue.

Two posts go further. The [patient communication post](/effective-patient-communication-techniques/) covers teach-back, where the patient repeats an instruction in their own words, which works as well at the desk as in the consulting room. The [negative reviews post](/maintaining-trust-how-to-handle-negative-reviews-in-healthcare-professionally/) covers what to say when a complaint goes public.

### Patient data and consent

Every role handles patient information, so every role needs the privacy rules: what may be said aloud at the counter, who may see which records, how to handle a request for a copy of a report, and what to do if a record goes to the wrong person.

In India, regulation 2.2 of the 2002 ethics regulations says confidences a patient entrusts to a physician should never be revealed unless the law requires it, and staff at the desk and in the consulting room hear some of them too. Consent belongs here too: regulation 7.16 says the physician should obtain consent in writing before performing an operation, so whoever prepares the consent form needs to know which procedures need one and who signs it. The [patient data privacy guide](/patient-data-privacy-laws-in-india/) covers the DPDP Act. In Kenya, section 42(4) of the [Data Protection Act, 2019](https://www.odpc.go.ke/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/TheDataProtectionAct__No24of2019.pdf) requires a data controller to take all reasonable steps to make sure the people it employs comply with its security measures, and training staff is an obvious place to start. The [Kenya data privacy post](/data-privacy-laws-in-kenya/) covers the rest of the Act.

## Build the plan in six steps

1. **Find the gaps.** Use staff reviews and patient feedback, and look at your own records: bills corrected after the patient left, appointments booked into the wrong diary, complaints that repeat and the questions the desk keeps asking the doctor. Break the needs down by role.
2. **Write a plan for the next three to six months.** Include induction for new staff, one topic a month for everyone, the checklists people will use afterwards, and a short check at the end of each topic.
3. **Pick trainers and mentors.** Pair each new person with a senior colleague in the same role. Rotate the training among senior staff so it does not always fall on one person.
4. **Choose how to deliver it.** A short session at the start of the day suits many topics. Record a walk-through of each routine task in your software once, and new staff can watch it again later.
5. **Check and give feedback.** Track who attended and check that they understood, by asking them to do the task, not only to describe it. Recognise good work, with a certificate or a bonus where you can afford one.
6. **Keep a training register.** Write down who was trained on what and when. In India the bio-medical waste rules ask for the number of staff trained and not trained in the annual report, and the register gives you those numbers.

## A yearly training calendar

| Training | For | What it covers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Induction | Every new person | Their own tasks and the software screens their role uses |
| Clinical skills | Doctors, nurses and lab staff | Protocols, and hands-on practice with the equipment |
| Software and admin | Front desk, billing and managers | Registration, appointments, bills and the day-end close |
| Bio-medical waste | Everyone who handles it | Segregation and storage, at induction and at least once a year in India |
| Emergency preparedness | All clinical staff | Life support, fire safety, outbreak procedures and the emergency kit |
| Refreshers | All staff | Changes to the rules and to your own processes |
| Leadership | Senior doctors and managers | Running the team and handling conflict |

## Training staff on new software

When a clinic changes its software, the records have to move and the habits have to change. Staff who knew the old system well are slower for a while in the new one, and staff who feel the new system adds steps may stop using it.

Two habits help. Train on real scenarios from your own clinic, such as a walk-in who needs a lab test and pays by card, instead of a tour of the menus. And train each role on its own screens. Warn everyone that the first days will feel slower, until the shortcuts become habit.

EasyClinic's training for a new clinic follows that pattern. Day 1 is basic training, day 2 is EMR scenario training, and days 3 and 4 are practice exercises and a session on getting to expert use, about two hours in all. Support by phone or in writing carries on afterwards on every plan, and Premium clinics also get a named account manager. The [switching page](/switch/) sets out the steps from the demo to the first day.

## Keep it going

Training works best as a habit. When the clinic pays for a course or a conference that fits someone's job, ask them to share what they learned at the next staff meeting. Keep the checklists and walk-through recordings in one shared folder, so the next new person starts from them.

When a rule changes or the software adds a screen, put it on the calendar as a refresher, and record it in the register like any other training.
