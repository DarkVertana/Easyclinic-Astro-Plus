Stock is cash on a shelf. In a clinic with its own dispensary or procedure room, medicines and supplies are a monthly bill like the rent, and part of that bill buys nothing: a box that expired on the shelf, or a dressing that was used and never billed.

This post goes through where that money goes and what to do about each part. Software helps with some of it, and the post says where.

## Where a clinic's stock money goes

The money goes in a few familiar places.

- Medicines and supplies that expire before anyone uses them.
- Stock bought in bulk that sits for months, holding cash the clinic could use elsewhere.
- Emergency buying when something runs out, from the chemist down the road at a retail price, or a patient sent away to buy it.
- Consumables used in a procedure that never reach the bill.
- Stock that goes missing between the register and the shelf.
- Items nobody prescribes any more, reordered out of habit.

Few of these show up as a line of their own in the accounts. Together they show up as a stock bill that is higher than the patient numbers suggest.

## Stop paying for stock that expires

Expired stock is the plainest loss, because the clinic paid for it and got nothing back. It tends to happen in one of two ways: a new delivery goes in front of the older batch, or the clinic bought more of a slow item than it could use before the date.

Dispense by expiry date, not by delivery date. This is first expiry, first out (FEFO), and it matters because a newer delivery can carry an earlier date than the stock already on the shelf. Put new deliveries behind the existing stock, and check the dates when you do.

Once a month, list everything due to expire in the next three months. Some of it can go back to the supplier if their terms allow returns. In a clinic with more than one branch, some of it can move to the branch that uses more of it. What cannot be used, stop ordering in that quantity.

## Set a reorder level for each item

Running out costs more than it looks. The patient is sent to the chemist, or the clinic buys a few strips at retail price to cover the gap until the delivery arrives.

A reorder level is the stock count at which you place the next order. A simple way to set one is:

**Reorder level = average daily use × supplier's delivery time in days + safety margin**

For example, if the clinic dispenses 10 strips of an antibiotic a day and the supplier takes 4 days to deliver, the clinic uses 40 strips while it waits. Add a margin for a busy week, say 20 strips, and the reorder level is 60. When the count falls to 60, the order goes out.

Start with the items that cost the most and the ones that move fastest, not every item on the shelf. Look at the levels again when patient numbers change, such as in the rainy season or the flu season, and when a supplier changes its delivery time.

Software makes this easier because the count follows each dispense. On EasyClinic the stock count is kept in real time as medicines are dispensed. The doctor also sees what is in stock while prescribing, so an item that has run out can be swapped for an alternative before the patient reaches the counter.

## Buy in bulk only what you will use before it expires

A bulk discount looks like a saving on the invoice. It is a saving only if the stock is used in time. A 10% discount is gone if a tenth of the order expires, and until the rest is used, it is cash the clinic cannot spend on rent or salaries.

Bulk buying makes sense for items with a long shelf life that the clinic uses every day. For anything slow-moving or short-dated, order smaller quantities more often, even at a slightly higher unit price.

## Bill the consumables you use

This is money lost on the other side of the counter. Dressings, sutures, syringes and gloves go into a procedure, and if nobody adds them to the bill, the clinic paid for them and the patient did not. It matters most where many visits involve a procedure, as in a dental or dermatology clinic.

There are two ways to fix it. Price the usual consumables into the procedure fee, or bill each one as a line of its own. Either way, check a few procedure bills against the stock used each month.

On EasyClinic, a consumable that is part of a service or a package comes off the stock count automatically with that service, so nobody has to enter it separately. ## Count the stock, and approve every adjustment

A stock count tells you whether the register and the shelf agree. A quick count of the costly and fast-moving items every month, and a full count once a quarter, is a workable rhythm for a small clinic.

When the count is short, someone has to write it off, and that is where stock can go missing without a trace. Decide who may adjust the stock, and require a second person to approve it. On EasyClinic, adjustments for shortfalls, excess, expired or damaged stock need authorisation and leave an audit log, and a report of fast- and slow-moving items shows what to stop reordering.

## If you run more than one branch

With two or more branches, one branch can hold too much of an item that another has run out of. Moving it across is cheaper than buying it fresh, as long as the transfer is recorded at both ends. On EasyClinic's Enterprise plan, stock moves between branches or out from a central warehouse, and each transfer is tracked until the receiving store books it in. ## Where to start

1. Count everything once, so the register matches the shelf.
2. List the items that cost the most and the ones that move fastest.
3. Set a reorder level for each item on that list.
4. Put a monthly expiry check in the diary.
5. Price the consumables into each procedure, or bill them as lines.
6. Review the slow-moving list every quarter and stop reordering what nobody uses.
