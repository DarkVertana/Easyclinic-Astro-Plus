Say a patient decides to book at ten at night, once the house is quiet. The clinic's number rings out, and by morning the patient has booked somewhere else or decided the cough can wait. Patient self-scheduling is the answer to that moment. The question for a clinic is whether that moment costs it enough patients to be worth changing how bookings are taken.

## Where phone booking loses patients

Phone booking asks the patient to act on the clinic's timetable, and each step can lose them.

- The line is engaged at the busiest hour of the morning, when the receptionist is also checking patients in.
- Nobody answers after closing time, which is when some patients have their first free minute.
- The receptionist checks the doctor's diary by hand while the patient waits on the line.
- Two people book the same slot, one by phone and one at the counter, and one of them finds out on the day.

None of these shows up in a report. The patient who gave up never appears in the appointment book, so the clinic cannot count what it lost.

## What changes when patients book themselves

The patient picks an open slot and gets a confirmation, at whatever hour suits them. The booking should land in the calendar the desk works from, beside the phone bookings and the walk-ins, and the patient should get a reminder before the visit like everyone else. Every booking made online is a call the desk did not have to take.

Walk-ins still come, and some patients will always phone. A self-booked patient can forget the visit like anyone else, so the reminder still matters.

## Where self-scheduling does not help

- **A clinic that runs mostly on walk-ins or tokens.** The queue is the booking, and an online slot adds little.
- **Visits that need a conversation first.** A new complaint that needs triage, or a procedure that needs preparation, is better booked by someone who can ask questions.
- **Patients who prefer to phone.** Keep the phone line and the counter open for them, on the same calendar.
- **Doctors whose hours change week to week.** Online slots are only as good as the schedule behind them. If the desk moves the doctor's hours by hand every Monday, fix that before you open the calendar to patients.

## What to open for self-booking first

Start with the visits that need no questions: follow-ups for patients the doctor already knows, with one doctor. Keep new complaints and procedures with the desk until you see how patients use it.

Ask only what you need to hold the slot, the patient's name and phone number, and collect the rest at the visit. Every extra field on a booking form is one more place for a patient on a phone to give up.

If you ask for payment when a patient books a long procedure slot, say before they pay what happens to the money if they cancel, and how late they can cancel.

## How to tell whether it pays off

Count the same things for the month before and the month after you open self-booking.

| What to count | Where to find it | What it tells you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bookings made online, as a share of all bookings | The appointment book | Whether patients use it at all |
| Online bookings made after closing time | The time each online booking was made | The bookings the phone line could not have taken |
| Booking calls to the desk each day | A tally sheet by the phone, kept for a week in each month | Whether the desk's load moved |
| No-shows among online bookings and among phone bookings | The appointment book | Whether self-booked patients turn up as reliably |
| Empty slots at the end of the day | The appointment book | Whether the extra bookings filled the diary |

If online bookings are a small share and almost none arrive after hours, the phone line was not your problem, and self-booking is a convenience rather than a source of new visits. If a fair share arrive after closing, those are patients the phone line could not have booked.

## A checklist before you switch it on

1. Pick one doctor, and the visit types patients may book themselves, such as follow-ups.
2. Make sure online bookings land in the calendar the desk books from, so nobody books the same slot twice.
3. Keep the booking to the patient's name and phone number.
4. Send a confirmation at once and a reminder before the visit.
5. Tell patients how to cancel or move a booking.
6. Tell the front desk what is changing, and who checks the online bookings each morning.
7. Count the numbers in the table above for a month, then decide whether to open more calendars.
