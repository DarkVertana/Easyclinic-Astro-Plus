A doctor who spends Sunday evening on payroll, or who chases a denied claim between patients, is doing work that someone outside the clinic could do. Outsourcing some of it can give that time back. The risk is handing over work you then stop watching, and patient data that leaves the building with it.

This post sets out which clinic work suits an outside firm, and what to put in the contract when you hand it over.

## Core and non-core work

Core work needs a clinician: diagnosis, treatment, prescribing and the conversation with the patient. It stays in-house, under your licensed team. Keep the front desk with it. The desk is where a patient decides whether the clinic is organised enough to come back to.

Non-core work keeps the clinic running without needing clinical judgement. It includes:

- Billing and insurance claims
- Payroll and HR paperwork
- IT support and backups
- Ordering stock and supplies
- Marketing and social media

Two questions sort each task. Can someone outside the clinic do it from written instructions, without a clinician deciding anything? And is the problem a skill nobody on your team has, or only the time nobody has? A task that passes the first question and needs a skill you would not hire full-time is the strongest candidate to outsource.

## What an outside firm can do well

**Skills you would not hire for.** Payroll rules and each insurer's claim rules are specialist knowledge, and so is network security. A small clinic rarely needs a full-time person for any of them, and a firm that does the same work for many clients may know them better.

**Cover when someone leaves.** When the one person who knows how to file claims resigns, the work stops. A firm keeps going through your staff changes and can take on more work in a busy season without you hiring.

**A cost you can plan.** You pay a fee for the work instead of a salary. Compare that fee with what the work costs you now in staff hours, including the doctor's own evenings, before you decide.

**Terms in writing.** A firm that signs a service level agreement (SLA) commits to turnaround times, which gives you something to hold it to. An employee's job description rarely does.

## The usual candidates, task by task

### Billing and insurance claims

Outsourced billing covers the claim work, from checking a patient's insurance cover to submitting and tracking the claim, and it can include invoicing patients and collecting what they owe. In a clinic where patients pay at the counter, the bill is raised at the desk anyway, so the part worth handing over is usually the claim work: submitting claims and following up denials.

Whoever does it, the owner should still see four things each month: visits with no bill against them, prices billed from the wrong list, cash that does not match the day's bills, and denied claims. The [revenue management page](/features/revenue-management/) shows how Easy Clinic reports each one. The denial and ageing reports need insurance claims, which is part of Enterprise and an add-on on Professional and Premium.

### IT support and backups

An IT firm can look after the computers, the printers, the network and the backups of anything stored at the clinic. Ask it to write down what it backs up and how quickly it can restore a machine. Ask your clinic software company the same about the patient records, so you know who covers what. The [cybersecurity post](/healthcare-cybersecurity-protecting-clinics-and-hospitals-in-the-digital-age/) covers testing a restore and the questions to ask your software vendor.

### Payroll and HR paperwork

A payroll firm can calculate salaries and deductions and keep the leave records. The clinic still decides who is hired and what they are paid, and the owner signs off each month's payroll before it is paid. Training in particular stays in-house; the [staff training post](/empowering-your-team-the-importance-of-staff-training-and-development-in-clinics/) sets out a plan by role.

### Stock and supplies

A supplier can take a standing order and deliver on a schedule. Counting the shelf and checking expiry dates still need someone on site. The [inventory costs post](/smarter-stocks-bigger-savings-how-clinic-inventory-management-helps-cut-costs/) covers what to check.

### Marketing

An agency can run the website and the social media accounts. In India, what it publishes in your name is still your publicity. Regulation 6.1.1 of the [2002 ethics regulations](https://www.nmc.org.in/page/rules-regulations-rules-regulations-of-erstwhile-mci-code-of-medical-ethics-regulations-2002), which the National Medical Commission [adopted in August 2023](https://www.nmc.org.in/storage/cms/rules-regulation-nmc/KEpwkMzgUwYNR49OrJaqvLcIpeytGPg7XGcorhTg.pdf), forbids a doctor to appear in publicity that invites attention to their skill or achievements, "either alone or in conjunction with others". Regulation 7.19 says a physician shall not use touts or agents for procuring patients. Read what the agency posts before it goes out, and if it wants to be paid per patient it brings in, ask your State Medical Council or a lawyer whether that fits the rules. The [clinic marketing strategy post](/clinic-marketing-strategy/) sets out what the regulations allow.

Appointment reminders do not need an agency. Clinic software sends them from the appointment book, and SMS and email reminders come with every Easy Clinic plan, with WhatsApp added on Premium.

## Before you sign

### Patient data leaves the building with the work

A billing firm reads diagnoses and bills, and an agency sending messages holds your patient list. Both handle patient data for you, and the law in India and in Kenya sets rules for how you hand it over.

In India, section 8 of the [Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2024/06/2bf1f0e9f04e6fb4f8fef35e82c42aa5.pdf) says a clinic may use a processor only under a valid contract, and that the clinic stays responsible for processing done on its behalf, irrespective of any agreement to the contrary. The DPDP Rules were notified in November 2025 with an [18-month phased compliance timeline](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2190014), and the [patient data privacy guide](/patient-data-privacy-laws-in-india/) explains what that means for a clinic. In Kenya, section 42 of the [Data Protection Act, 2019](https://www.odpc.go.ke/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/TheDataProtectionAct__No24of2019.pdf) asks the clinic to choose a processor that gives sufficient guarantees about the organisational measures the Act requires, and to sign a written contract under which the processor acts only on the clinic's instructions. The [Kenya data privacy post](/data-privacy-laws-in-kenya/) covers the rest.

In practice, that means:

- A written contract that says what data the firm may see and use, and what it must do if the data leaks.
- Data sent to the firm over an encrypted connection, not as a plain email attachment.
- A separate login for each person at the firm, never a shared password, so their access can be limited and their work can be traced. On Easy Clinic, each person logs in as themselves and the role on the login decides which screens it opens. Audit logs record what each user did.
- Access removed the day the contract ends, and the firm's copies of your data returned or deleted.

### Put the service in writing

Before you sign, get the scope and the turnaround times in writing, along with what the firm reports and how often, who to call when something goes wrong, and how either side ends the contract. Ask whether it has worked with clinics before, and ask for two clinics you can call.

| Risk | What to put in place |
| --- | --- |
| You stop seeing the work | A monthly report, and one person at the clinic who reads it |
| Patient data leaves the clinic | A written contract, a login for each person, access removed at the end |
| The firm does not know your software | Ask before signing whether it has worked in it, and give it only the screens it needs |
| Your records stay with the firm | Agree in the contract how your data is returned or deleted when you part |

### Name one person to manage the firm

Outsourced work still needs an owner inside the clinic. Pick one person to be the firm's contact. Agree the measures you will judge the firm on, such as claims submitted within an agreed number of days, and review them together every month for the first quarter and every quarter after that.

## When to keep work in-house

Keep the work in-house when it involves the patient directly or needs a clinical decision. Keep it too when doing it yourself is cheaper once you count the time it takes to manage a firm. A large hospital may find a full-time IT team cheaper than a contract. A one-doctor clinic has far less IT work to fill a full-time job.

Consider a mix: the front desk and the consulting room stay in-house, and the repetitive work behind them goes to firms the owner watches.

## How to start

1. **List the work.** Write down every non-clinical task for a month, with who does it and how long it takes.
2. **Pick one task.** Start with the one that costs the doctor the most time or needs the skill nobody has.
3. **Talk to more than one firm.** Ask each for references from clinics and for its terms in writing.
4. **Agree the measures.** Put the turnaround times, the reports and the data terms in the contract.
5. **Review it.** Check the reports every month, and decide at the end of the first quarter whether to keep the firm or bring the work back.
