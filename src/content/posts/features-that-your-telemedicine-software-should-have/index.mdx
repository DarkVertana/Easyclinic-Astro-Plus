If you have tried video consults on your own phone, you know it works for a while. The trouble starts when the clinic needs a record of the call or a copy of the prescription. This checklist is what to test before you pay for telemedicine software. It applies to any vendor, Easy Clinic included.

## Read your country's rules first

The rules decide which consults you may hold by video, and so which features you need. In Uganda, for example, the [Uganda Medical and Dental Practitioners Council](https://umdpc.go.ug/downloads/requirements/requirements.pdf) allows telemedicine only as part of a clinic it has licensed, and only for patients seen in person by the same practice within the previous 12 months. Our post on [telemedicine in Uganda](/healthcare-in-uganda/) sets out those rules, and for India the [telehealth page](/features/telehealth/) summarises the telemedicine practice guidelines. Check what your own rules say a remote prescription must carry before you look at any software.

## 1. The video call

- Does the patient have to install an app, or can they join from a browser on their phone? An app to install is one more step for the patient before the call starts.
- Test it from the patient's side, on a cheap phone using mobile data, rather than on the clinic's Wi-Fi.
- What happens when the line drops? Can the patient rejoin the same consult, or do you start again?
- Does the call run inside the software, or does the software hand you off to a separate video app whose history the clinic does not keep?

## 2. The prescription

- Is the prescription written on the same patient record as clinic visits, so it is on screen at the next visit?
- Does it show that the consult was held remotely, and does it carry what your rules ask of a prescription?
- How does it reach the patient? A typed prescription on WhatsApp is easier for the pharmacist to read than a photo of the pad.
- Does the clinic keep a copy, and can the patient print it or show it at any pharmacy?

## 3. The fee

- Can the patient pay when they book, or only after the call? Chasing a fee after the call is a conversation nobody at the clinic wants, so paying at booking is simpler.
- What does each payment cost? Ask for the payment gateway's charge and any commission the vendor adds on top, in writing.
- How soon does the money reach your bank account, and who handles the refund when the doctor has to cancel?
- Does the payment land on the patient's bill in the software, with a receipt, so the day's accounts match?

## 4. Scheduling and the follow-up

- Are video consults on the same calendar as clinic visits, so nobody books a video patient into a slot where the doctor is with someone in the room?
- Does the patient get a reminder before the call, and on which channel?
- Can the desk book the follow-up before the call ends, as a clinic visit or another video consult?
- Can patients book a video slot themselves, and if so, from where?

## 5. The record and the data

- Do the notes and the prescription from a video consult sit on the patient's record, next to their clinic visits?
- Who at the clinic can open the notes from a video consult?
- In which country is the data hosted, and can you take a full export if you leave?
- Does your country restrict storing health data abroad? If it does, the hosting answer decides which approvals you need before you start.

## 6. The price and the plan

Video is sometimes sold as part of a higher plan rather than on its own. Compare the price per doctor per month with video included, rather than the entry price. Ask whether online payments and WhatsApp messages come in the same plan, because you will probably want the fee paid online and the prescription sent on WhatsApp.
