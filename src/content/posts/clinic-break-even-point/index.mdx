This post is for the owner of a small clinic, usually the doctor, who wants one number to watch in the first year. It shows the calculation, then the two refinements that change the answer most. Every figure in the examples is a round number chosen to show the arithmetic, so put your own in their place.

## The formula

Break-even patients a month = fixed monthly costs ÷ (average income per patient − cost per patient)

Fixed costs are the ones you pay whether ten patients come or two hundred: rent, salaries, electricity and internet, software, marketing, and the instalment on any loan you took to open. Include the loan, because it leaves the bank account every month like the rent does.

Average income per patient is the fees collected in the month divided by the patients seen. Count collections, not bills: a bill that is still unpaid at the end of the month has not paid the landlord. Count free follow-ups as patients too, because they take a slot and bring in nothing, which pulls the average down.

Cost per patient is whatever goes out with each visit. For most small clinics that is consumables, such as gloves and dressings. It can be much larger, as the fee-share example below shows.

If you want a quick first answer, skip the cost per patient and divide all the month's costs by the average fee. That is what the first example does.

## A worked example in rupees

These are illustrative figures for a small single-doctor clinic in India, not the books of a real clinic.

| Monthly cost | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Rent | ₹45,000 |
| Staff salaries | ₹70,000 |
| Electricity, water and internet | ₹10,000 |
| Consumables and admin | ₹20,000 |
| Marketing and local promotion | ₹15,000 |
| Software and other tools | ₹10,000 |
| Total | ₹1,70,000 |

With an average of ₹700 collected per patient, the clinic needs ₹1,70,000 ÷ ₹700 = 242.9 patients a month. Round up, because 242 patients would leave the month a little short, and the answer is 243. Over 26 working days that is 9.3 a day, so the clinic needs 10 patients on an ordinary day to cover its month.

The average matters as much as the count. If the same clinic collected ₹900 a patient instead of ₹700, break-even would fall to 189 patients a month, just over 7 a day. The average moves when the mix of visits changes: fewer free follow-ups, more procedures, a fee revision or fewer unpaid bills all push it up.

## Separate the costs that come with each patient

The first answer treats consumables as a fixed cost, but they rise and fall with the number of patients. Suppose the ₹20,000 works out at about ₹80 a visit. The fixed costs are then ₹1,50,000, and each patient leaves ₹700 − ₹80 = ₹620 towards them. Break-even is ₹1,50,000 ÷ ₹620 = 241.9, so 242 patients. Almost no change, because consumables are small next to the fee.

It changes a great deal when a doctor is paid a share of each fee instead of a salary. Suppose a visiting consultant takes 40% of each ₹700 fee, which is ₹280. Each patient now leaves ₹700 − ₹80 − ₹280 = ₹340, and break-even is ₹1,50,000 ÷ ₹340 = 441.2, so 442 patients a month. That is nearly double. A fee share can still be the right arrangement when the consultant brings in patients the clinic would not otherwise see, but work out the new break-even before you agree to it.

## Earning back the setup money comes after break-even

Break-even covers the month. The money you spent to open, on the deposit and the fit-out, comes back only from the surplus above break-even.

Take the first example and suppose the clinic sees 300 patients a month, about 11.5 a day. It collects 300 × ₹700 = ₹2,10,000, which leaves ₹40,000 after the ₹1,70,000 of costs. If the setup cost ₹10 lakh, it takes ₹10,00,000 ÷ ₹40,000 = 25 months to earn it back.

One more patient a day changes that more than you might expect. Twenty-six more patients a month adds ₹18,200, the surplus becomes ₹58,200, and the ₹10 lakh comes back in just over 17 months instead of 25. For what the setup money actually goes on, [the Mumbai cost guide](/how-much-does-it-cost-to-open-a-clinic-in-mumbai/) lists it line by line.

## The figures to check at the end of each month

- Patients seen, and how many of them paid nothing.
- Fees collected, and the bills still unpaid.
- The average collected per patient, and whether it moved since last month.
- Any cost that changed, such as a rent increase or a new salary.

If the average falls, look at the free follow-ups and the unpaid bills before you look for more patients. If a cost rises, work out the new break-even the same day, because a ₹10,000 rise in rent at ₹620 a patient means 17 more patients every month.
