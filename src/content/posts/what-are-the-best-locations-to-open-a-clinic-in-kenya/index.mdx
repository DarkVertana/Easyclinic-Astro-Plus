Choosing a place for a clinic in Kenya comes down to two questions: are there enough patients within reach, and is there a gap you can fill? Two public data sets help you shortlist before you spend money on visits. One is the population density of each county from the 2019 census. The other is the map of malaria zones from the 2020 Kenya Malaria Indicator Survey. Neither replaces walking the street, and the last section of this post is what to look for when you do.

## Start with how many people live within reach

The [2019 Kenya Population and Housing Census](https://www.knbs.or.ke/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/2019-Kenya-population-and-Housing-Census-Volume-1-Population-By-County-And-Sub-County.pdf) gives the number of people per square kilometre in every county. These were the five densest:

| County | People per square kilometre (2019) |
| --- | --- |
| Nairobi City | 6,247 |
| Mombasa | 5,495 |
| Vihiga | 1,047 |
| Kisii | 958 |
| Kiambu | 952 |

The sparsest counties in the same table are in the north, such as Marsabit and Turkana.

A dense county puts many patients within walking or matatu distance. It also puts more clinics and chemists within the same distance, and the rent is usually higher. A sparse county works the other way round: the patients you serve may live many kilometres apart, so a fixed clinic serves a very wide area, and outreach days may reach more people than the clinic door.

A county figure also hides the street. The same census gives a density for every sub-county, and Nairobi's sub-counties differ widely from one another. Once you have a county in mind, look at the sub-county table in the same report, and then at the estate itself.

## Match the clinic to the illnesses around it

What patients come in with changes from one part of Kenya to another, and malaria shows it most clearly. The [2020 Kenya Malaria Indicator Survey](https://dhsprogram.com/pubs/pdf/MIS36/MIS36.pdf), run by the Ministry of Health's Division of National Malaria Programme and KNBS, splits the country into five malaria zones and tested children aged 6 months to 14 in each:

| Malaria zone | Counties | Children with malaria (microscopy, 2020) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lake endemic | Siaya, Kisumu, Migori, Homa Bay, Vihiga, Busia, and parts of Kakamega and Bungoma | 18.9% |
| Coast endemic | Mombasa, Kwale, Kilifi, Lamu, Taita Taveta | 4.5% |
| Seasonal | Tana River, Marsabit, Isiolo, Kitui, Garissa, Wajir, Mandera, Turkana, Samburu, Baringo, Kajiado, and parts of West Pokot, Elgeyo Marakwet, Meru, Tharaka-Nithi and Embu | 1.8% |
| Highland epidemic prone | Kisii, Nyamira, Trans-Nzoia, Uasin Gishu, Nandi, Narok, Kericho, Bomet, and parts of West Pokot, Bungoma, Kakamega and Elgeyo Marakwet | 0.7% |
| Low risk | Nairobi, Nyandarua, Nyeri, Kirinyaga, Murang'a, Kiambu, Machakos, Makueni, Laikipia, Nakuru, and parts of Meru, Tharaka-Nithi and Embu | 0.4% |

Across Kenya the figure was 5.6%. In the lake zone malaria will be a regular part of the work, so plan the test kits and the pharmacy stock around it. In the low-risk zone around Nairobi and central Kenya it is rare in children, and the stock can be planned around other conditions. The highland zone needs care of its own. The [survey report](https://dhsprogram.com/pubs/pdf/MIS36/MIS36.pdf) describes transmission in the western highlands as seasonal, with considerable year-to-year variation, and with epidemics when minimum temperatures stay high during the long and short rains, so a clinic in Kisii or Kericho keeps test kits in stock even in a quiet month.

The survey is about malaria. For the rest of the local pattern of illness, ask the chemists and the clinicians already working in the area what they see most, and ask at the sub-county health office when you go to introduce yourself.

## Check the roads patients will use

A clinic is only as near as the road to it. One measure of that is the Rural Access Index, which the UN's [indicator definition](https://unstats.un.org/sdgs/metadata/files/Metadata-09-01-01.pdf) describes as the share of the rural population living within 2 km of an all-season road. The Kenya Roads Board publishes a [Rural Access Index map of Kenya](https://maps.krb.go.ke/kenya-roads-board12769/maps/119381/7-rural-access-index), built from its own road data and KNBS population figures, where you can look up your county.

Then check the road yourself. Walk it from the nearest matatu stage and time it, and if you can, go back during the rains, when a murram road that is easy in the dry season can be hard going.

## What changes with the place: the rent and the land

The location changes what the clinic costs to open as well as who walks in. The [Nairobi cost guide](/how-much-does-it-cost-to-open-a-clinic-in-nairobi/) builds a small clinic's budget line by line in KES, and the lines that change with the site are the land and the rent. Start from that budget and replace those two lines with the figures for your own site.

## Check the street before you sign

1. Shortlist two or more sub-counties where the density and the malaria zone fit the clinic you plan to run.
2. Visit each site on a market day and on a quiet day, in the morning and in the evening.
3. Count the clinics, dispensaries, chemists and labs within walking distance, and ask what each one offers.
4. Check the water, the electricity, the toilet and how you will dispose of clinical waste, which the guide to [starting a clinic in Kenya](/the-ultimate-guide-to-starting-a-clinic-in-kenya/) covers with the inspection.
5. Check the mobile signal inside the building and whether a fixed internet line reaches it.
6. Introduce yourself at the sub-county health office before you sign a lease.
7. Set the rent against the number of patients you can realistically see there.
