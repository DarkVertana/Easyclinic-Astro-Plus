A consultation can go one way: the patient comes with a worry, hears an explanation, nods, and goes home unsure what to do. The same gap opens at the desk when the fasting instructions for tomorrow's test are read out once over a queue.

This post is for the doctors and the desk staff. It follows the health literacy toolkit that the US Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) publishes for practices, because it is specific and public, and it adds what a clinic can put in writing.

## Start by listening

Open with a question the patient cannot answer in one word, such as "Tell me what has been happening since the last visit", and let them finish. The [AHRQ toolkit](https://www.ahrq.gov/health-literacy/improve/precautions/tool4.html) asks you to sit at the patient's level and look at them, not at the computer, and to ask clarifying questions rather than interrupt.

Say what you notice as well as what you hear. A patient at a first visit, or one who has just heard a new diagnosis, is often frightened, and saying so ("This is a lot to take in") costs a few seconds. Sensitive conversations need a place where the next patient cannot hear them, which in a small clinic may mean closing the door or keeping the corridor clear, and the desk should know when not to knock.

## Explain in the patient's words

Notice the words the patient uses for their illness and use them back. Replace medical terms with the ones you would use with your own family: "sugar" before "glycaemic control", "tummy" before "abdomen". Where the patient needs to learn a term, ask what they know about it first. The AHRQ toolkit's example is HbA1c, explained as a test that shows how much sugar has been in the blood over the past few months.

Be specific. "Take one tablet after breakfast and one after dinner" is clearer than "twice a day", and "come back if the fever lasts past Friday" is clearer than "if it doesn't settle". Draw a picture on the prescription pad, or show the exercise, when words are not enough.

## Check understanding with teach-back

Teach-back means asking the patient to tell you the plan in their own words. The point, as the [AHRQ toolkit](https://www.ahrq.gov/health-literacy/improve/precautions/tool5.html) puts it, is to check how well you explained, not to test the patient, so the wording carries the blame:

- "I want to make sure I explained this clearly. Can you tell me how you will take the new tablets?"
- "When you get home, what will you tell your family about today?"

"Do you understand?" and "Does that make sense?" are not teach-back questions, because patients say yes either way. If the answer shows a gap, explain it a different way and ask again. For a long consultation, the toolkit suggests "chunk and check": explain one part, check it, then move to the next, rather than saving the check for the end.

The toolkit's advice for starting is to try it with the last patient of the day, and to use it with everyone once it feels natural. It says that once teach-back is routine it does not lengthen a visit.

## Use show-me for medicines and devices

Some instructions are better shown than told. When you start or change a medicine, or hand over an inhaler or an insulin pen, ask the patient to show you how they will use it. The [AHRQ toolkit](https://www.ahrq.gov/health-literacy/improve/precautions/tool5.html) notes that many patients who can say the dose correctly still make mistakes when asked to demonstrate it.

## Ask for questions the right way

"Do you have any questions?" invites a no. The [AHRQ toolkit](https://www.ahrq.gov/health-literacy/improve/precautions/tool14.html) suggests "What questions do you have?", asked more than once during the visit, so the patient hears that you expect questions. Sit down and do not stand by the door while you ask it.

The desk can help at both ends. At check-in, tell the patient to ask the doctor anything they want to, and offer paper to write the questions down. At check-out, ask whether all their questions were answered, and know whom to fetch if the answer is no.

## Put the plan in writing

The patient forgets part of any conversation, so what they take home should carry the plan: the medicines with doses and timing, the tests, what to watch for and when to come back. A clear prescription does most of this.

On Easy Clinic, the note and the prescription go on one patient record, and the prescription is printed for the patient or, on the Premium plan, sent on WhatsApp, so the dosage instructions are on the patient's phone at the pharmacy counter. Clinics in Kenya can issue prescriptions in English and Swahili. Wherever you practise, the written plan should be in a language the patient reads, and where the doctor and patient do not share a language, the [AHRQ toolkit](https://www.ahrq.gov/health-literacy/improve/precautions/tool4.html) advises a qualified interpreter over a relative or an untrained member of staff.

## Decide together

When there is more than one reasonable treatment, set out the options with what each involves and costs, and what could go wrong, and ask what matters to the patient: work, travel, money, family. Then check the decision with teach-back.

Consent is where this matters most. The [AHRQ toolkit](https://www.ahrq.gov/health-literacy/improve/precautions/tool5.html) recommends making teach-back part of every informed consent discussion. In India, regulation 7.16 of the [Indian Medical Council (Professional Conduct, Etiquette and Ethics) Regulations, 2002](https://nmc.org.in/page/rules-regulations-rules-regulations-of-erstwhile-mci-code-of-medical-ethics-regulations-2002), which the National Medical Commission [adopted in August 2023](https://www.nmc.org.in/storage/cms/rules-regulation-nmc/KEpwkMzgUwYNR49OrJaqvLcIpeytGPg7XGcorhTg.pdf), says the doctor should obtain consent in writing before performing an operation. Check with your State Medical Council for any later change before you rely on it.

## The front desk and the phone

Patients meet the desk before they meet the doctor, and they judge the clinic by both. The same habits apply at the counter:

- Greet each patient and tell them roughly how long the wait is. If the doctor is running late, say so and say why.
- Use teach-back on the practical instructions: the arrival time, fasting before a test, which medicines to bring and the details of a referral, which the AHRQ toolkit names as things appointment staff can check.
- Write the phrases for the hard conversations in advance: a doctor running an hour late, a bill higher than the patient expected, a report that is not ready and a patient who wants the doctor without an appointment. Practise them in pairs at a staff meeting.
- After a procedure or a difficult diagnosis, have someone call the patient the next day to ask how they are and whether anything in the plan is unclear.

Reminders are part of the same conversation. Where you can set their wording, write it the way the desk would say it, and the [AHRQ toolkit](https://www.ahrq.gov/health-literacy/improve/precautions/tool14.html) suggests a reminder can also ask the patient to bring a written list of questions.

## Ask patients how it went

You find out whether the communication worked by asking. A short survey after the visit gives an unhappy patient somewhere to say so before they tell anyone else, and it shows which part of the visit to fix first. Easy Clinic's patient engagement tools include a feedback survey after the visit, whose ratings come back to the clinic to act on. Read the comments together once a month, and change one script or one habit at a time.

## What software does not do

Software keeps the written plan and sends the reminders. It does not listen, or notice that a patient has gone quiet. Those stay with the doctor and the desk, and they improve with practice rather than with a new tool.
