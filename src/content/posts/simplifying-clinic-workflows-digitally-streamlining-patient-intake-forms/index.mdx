A paper intake form gets written twice. The patient fills it in standing in the queue, with a borrowed pen, and later someone at the desk types it into the system, guessing at the handwriting. A digital intake form moves the first job to the patient's own phone, before the visit, and when the answers go straight onto the record, it removes the second.

This post covers the questions to put on the form and the ones to leave off. Then it sets out how to move a clinic from the clipboard to a link without leaving any patient unable to register.

## What makes an intake form digital

A digital intake form is a web form. The clinic sends the patient a link, by SMS or WhatsApp for example, when the visit is booked, and the patient fills it in on their phone at home. The answers arrive as data the clinic's software can put on the patient's record.

A PDF sent on WhatsApp is not the same thing. The patient has to print it or fill it in on screen, then send back a photo or a file, and someone at the desk still types the answers in. The retyping is where a misread allergy or a wrong phone number gets in, so a form that still needs retyping has moved the clipboard onto a phone and changed little else.

## What to put on the form

Nine sections cover what a general clinic needs at a first visit. Each row says why the answer matters, so you can drop a row your clinic has no use for.

| Section | What to ask | Why the clinic needs it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Identity | Full name as it appears on the patient's ID, date of birth, sex and preferred language | Finding the right record at the next visit, and not opening a second one for the same patient |
| Contact | Mobile number and whether it is on WhatsApp, plus email if they use it | Where reminders and reports will go |
| Someone to call | A name and phone number, and their relationship to the patient | Reaching family if something goes wrong during or after the visit |
| Why they are coming | The problem in their own words, and how long it has been going on | The doctor reads it before calling the patient in |
| Allergies | Any allergy to medicines, and what happened | Checked before anything is prescribed |
| Current medicines | Names and doses, including medicines from other doctors and anything bought at the chemist without a prescription | Checking for interactions, and knowing what the patient already takes |
| Past health | Long-term conditions such as diabetes or high blood pressure, and past operations or hospital stays | The history the first consult would otherwise have to rebuild |
| Paying | Self-pay or insurance, and for insured patients the insurer and member number | Raising the bill, or the claim, correctly the first time |
| Messages and consent | Which messages the patient agrees to receive, asking about reminders and reports separately from offers, and the consent your country's law asks for | What the clinic may send, and a record of what the patient agreed to |

A specialist adds only the questions the doctor reads at the first visit, such as the date of the last period at a gynaecology clinic. Everything else can wait for the consult.

## What to leave off

Leave off any question that nobody at the clinic reads. Paper forms collect questions over the years, such as a field for an insurer the clinic no longer works with, or the same allergy question asked on two pages. Every one of them is one more reason for the patient to give up on their phone and leave the form for the desk.

Keep sensitive numbers out of the chat. If the link goes by WhatsApp, the answers belong in the form, never typed into the conversation. WhatsApp's own [Business Messaging Policy](https://whatsappbusiness.com/policy/) tells businesses not to ask people to share full payment card numbers, financial account numbers, ID card numbers or other sensitive identifiers on WhatsApp.

Leave off consent wording copied from a form written for another country. Write the consent questions for the law where your clinic practises. The [patient engagement page](/features/patient-engagement/#consent-by-country) sets out, in general terms, what the laws of India, Kenya, the UAE and Nigeria ask of a clinic that messages its patients.

## Moving from the clipboard to a link, in five steps

### 1. Collect every form in use

Gather the registration form, the history sheet, the consent page and anything else a new patient fills in. List every question, and next to each one write who reads the answer and when. The questions with nobody against them are the ones to drop.

### 2. Build the form with the doctor

Sit with the doctor and decide which answers are required and which are optional. Keep the required ones to what the desk cannot register a patient without, and what the doctor needs before the consult. The allergy question stays required.

### 3. Test it on an ordinary phone

Fill the form in on an inexpensive phone on mobile data, not on the office computer. Then hand it to someone who did not build it, such as a relative of a staff member, and watch where they stop to ask a question. Each of those places needs a clearer question.

### 4. Send the link when the visit is booked

The booking is the natural moment, because the patient is already on the phone or on WhatsApp with the clinic. Send it again with the appointment reminder to patients who have not filled it in, and tell them in the message how long it takes.

### 5. Keep paper at the desk

Some patients will always register at the desk, such as walk-ins and anyone without a smartphone or without data that day. Keep the paper form or a tablet for them, and have the receptionist enter those answers the same day, so the doctor sees every new patient's form in one place.

## What the desk still does

A form filled in at home is only as reliable as what the patient remembered. When the patient arrives, the receptionist checks the name against their ID and the phone number against the phone in their hand, because a misspelt name can become a second record for the same patient. The doctor or nurse asks the allergy question again out loud.

The receptionist's time at registration now goes on checking what the patient typed.

## Questions to ask about any intake software

Before you choose a system, ask the vendor these, and ask to see each one happen:

- Do the answers go on the patient record, or into a separate inbox that someone copies from?
- Can the clinic change the questions itself, and who does it when a doctor wants a new one?
- Can the link go by SMS as well as WhatsApp, and which plan does each need?
- What happens to a form the patient starts and does not finish?
- Where are the answers stored, and in which country?
