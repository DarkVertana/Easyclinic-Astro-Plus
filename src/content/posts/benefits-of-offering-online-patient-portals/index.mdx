A patient portal is a login for your patients. Through it they see their own records from your clinic and deal with the desk online, from booking the next visit to paying a bill. It can be a website or a phone app. This post covers what a portal does for a clinic and how to tell whether yours needs one.

## What patients use a portal for

Most portals offer some mix of these:

- Their records: past visits, prescriptions, lab results and the treatment plan the doctor wrote.
- Booking: choosing a slot for the next visit, or moving one they cannot make.
- Repeat prescriptions: asking for a refill by message instead of by phone.
- Payments: paying a bill online, and on some portals ordering medicines.
- Forms: filling in the registration or history form at home before the first visit.

The patients who get the most from a portal are the ones who come back. A patient with hypertension who wants last month's report, or a mother checking her antenatal scan, has a reason to log in. A patient who came once with a sprained ankle does not.

## What it takes off the front desk

Think about the calls your receptionist takes on an ordinary morning. "Is my report ready?" "Can I move Thursday's appointment?" "I need my tablets again." "How much do I still owe?" Each one means finding the file and calling back, sometimes after waiting for the doctor. In a portal, the patient opens the report or picks the new slot without anyone at the desk.

Forms are the other saving. A form the patient fills in at home arrives typed, so nobody guesses at handwriting or keys it in twice, and the allergy the patient wrote down reaches the chart as they wrote it.

We have no measured figure for how many calls a portal saves a clinic, so this post gives none. If you want your own number, tally the desk's calls for a week by what the patient wanted, before and after you offer a portal.

## What a portal does not do

- It does not reach patients who never log in. Some of your patients will not, and you cannot run the clinic's reminders through a login they do not use.
- It does not replace reminders. A reminder has to arrive on the patient's phone without them opening anything, so reminders go out as messages, by SMS and email or on WhatsApp.
- It does not answer clinical questions. A refill request still needs a doctor to decide, and someone has to read the messages every day and reply, or patients will phone anyway.
- It adds work at the desk, from signing patients up to resetting forgotten passwords.

## Is a portal worth it for your clinic?

It is more likely to be worth it when:

- Many of your patients return on a schedule, for chronic care, antenatal visits, physiotherapy courses or reviews after a procedure.
- Your desk spends a large part of the day on report and refill calls.
- You run more than one branch, and patients book at whichever one is nearer.
- You already produce lab reports and want patients to find the old ones themselves.

It is less likely to be worth it when most of your patients are walk-ins seen once, or when few of them use smartphones. In both cases, messages reach more of your patients than a login would.

## If you offer one, make it worth opening

A portal that nobody logs in to still costs the same. Some habits that give patients a reason to use it:

- Tell every patient about it at the desk, and give them the link when they pay.
- Put the things patients want there first, such as the lab report and the next appointment.
- Answer portal messages the same day, so patients learn that writing works.
- Keep the phone line open. Patients who prefer to call should still get through.

## Patient data in a portal

A portal puts health records on a patient's phone, so read what your country's data protection law asks before you offer one. The [patient engagement page](/features/patient-engagement/) sets out, in general terms, what the data protection laws of India, Kenya, the UAE and Nigeria ask of a clinic that messages its patients, with a link to each law.

Before you offer a portal, ask any vendor how patients log in, what a patient sees if they share a phone with family, who at the clinic can read portal messages, and what happens to the login when a patient stops coming to your clinic.
