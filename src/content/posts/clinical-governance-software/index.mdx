With one clinic, the owner sees how every doctor works, because the owner works beside them. From the second branch on, that stops. Each branch builds its own habits, and the protocol written at head office is followed closely where it was written and loosely three branches away.

This post is for the owner or medical director of a clinic chain who wants a patient to get the same standard of care at every branch. It ends with a pilot on one pathway that a chain can start next month.

## Why care drifts from branch to branch

Drift does not need a careless doctor. It needs a protocol kept in a PDF that nobody opens, and a busy afternoon. A new doctor learns the branch's habits rather than the protocol, and a check skipped once is skipped again, with nobody at head office placed to notice.

It shows up in the same places:

- A check before a procedure that one branch always records and another rarely does.
- A follow-up that is booked before the patient leaves at one branch and left to the patient at another.
- Notes that differ so much from doctor to doctor that a second doctor cannot pick up the case.
- No record of who did a step, or when.

Each gap is small on its own. Across a chain they add up to care that depends on which door the patient walked through, and head office may hear about it first from a complaint.

## What clinical governance means in a chain

Clinical governance is the system that lets head office check the standard of care instead of assuming it. In a chain it has two halves: the standard, written down as clinical SOPs, and the evidence that each branch follows it.

The work runs as a loop. Head office writes the SOP with the doctors who will use it, and the branches work from it. Adherence is measured from the patient record. Once a month a named clinician reviews the figures, and either the SOP changes or the branch gets training.

## Write SOPs a doctor will use

A clinical SOP sets the minimum a patient on one pathway should get at any branch. It is short enough to follow in a busy clinic, and it leaves the clinical decision to the doctor. A useful one does four things:

- It names the pathway and the patients it applies to.
- It lists the steps that must be recorded, which for a diabetes review might include the foot check and the HbA1c.
- It says when to escalate or refer, and to whom.
- It says where a doctor may depart from it, and asks for the reason to be noted.

The last point keeps the doctors on side. An SOP that allows a recorded departure shows head office where the protocol does not fit real patients, and that is how the protocol gets better.

Put the steps where the work happens. A step in the note template is in front of the doctor during the consult, and a step in a policy manual is not. ## Measure adherence from the record

A manual audit pulls a sample of files after the fact. It takes a person's time, and it describes last quarter. If the SOP's steps are recorded in the chart, adherence can be counted from the record instead.

For each required step, adherence is the share of eligible visits in which the step was recorded. Count it by branch and by month, and look at individual doctors only when a branch figure points you there.

Read the figures for patterns before people. When every doctor at one branch skips the same step, look for a cause at the branch first, such as a missing instrument or a template that hides the field. When one doctor skips it at every branch they work in, that is a conversation with the doctor.

A recorded step is not proof that the step was done well. Keep a small monthly sample of cases that a senior clinician reads in full, beside the adherence figures.

## A pilot on one pathway

A chain does not need to standardise everything at once. Start with one pathway at a few branches, and show it works before adding the next.

1. **Choose the pathway.** Pick one that many patients go through at every branch and where a skipped step can harm the patient, such as the diabetes review or the prescribing of a high-risk medicine.
2. **Write the SOP with the branch doctors.** Agree the required steps and the point at which to escalate, and put the steps into the note template.
3. **Measure where you start.** Before the SOP goes live, read a sample of recent visits at each pilot branch and record how often each step was done.
4. **Review it every month.** Compare adherence with the starting figure. Where the doctors show the SOP does not fit, change it, and where a step is still missed, train. Add the next branch or the next pathway once the first is being followed.

## Who owns governance in a chain

Give it named owners. A clinician at head office owns the SOP library and the monthly review. Each branch has a lead doctor who answers for that branch's adherence and brings back what does not work.

Put a date and a version on every SOP, and tell every branch in writing when one changes. A change that reaches four branches out of five is a new source of drift.

A new branch should start on the current SOPs from its first day. On Easy Clinic's Enterprise plan, head office's SOPs are in place for a new branch before its first patient, and its staff take the same four-day training as every other branch.

## Signs a chain needs this now

- Patients at one branch complain about something the others do not.
- Notes look so different between branches that a doctor covering another branch cannot pick up a case.
- Nobody can say whether last month's change to a protocol reached every branch.
- The only way to check a protocol is to pull files by hand.

## What software cannot do

Software can put the SOP's steps in front of the doctor and count whether they were recorded. It cannot tell a careful examination from a ticked box, and it cannot write a good SOP for you. Too many required steps can backfire as well: a doctor may start clicking through them, and then the adherence figure can rise while the care does not. Keep the required steps few, and read real cases every month.
