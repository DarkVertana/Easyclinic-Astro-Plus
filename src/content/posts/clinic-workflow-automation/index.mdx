Most of a clinic's day is small jobs done again and again: reminding tomorrow's patients, booking the follow-up, chasing a bill that was not paid, and adding up the takings before locking up. Clinic workflow automation means the software does some of those steps without anyone clicking a button. This post sets out which jobs to hand over first and which to keep with people. It ends with the question owners ask less often than they should, which is who answers for it when the software gets something wrong.

## Rule-based automation and AI

Automation in a clinic comes in two kinds. The older kind follows a rule the clinic sets, such as "send a reminder the day before every booked appointment". It does the same thing every time, and when something falls outside the rule, such as a patient replying with a question, a person takes over.

The newer kind uses AI to read something, such as a consult conversation or a patient's message, and draft or suggest what happens next. Some vendors now describe "AI agents" that carry a whole task through on their own. Whatever the label, ask two questions of any tool: what does it do without a person, and what does it hand back to one?

## What to automate first

Good first jobs share four traits. They repeat many times a day, they follow the same steps each time, a mistake is cheap and easy to spot, and a person can check the result in a few minutes.

| Job | Why it suits automation | What a person still does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Appointment reminders | Every booked patient needs one, and only the name and the time change | Calls the patients who have not replied, if the clinic asks for a reply |
| Follow-up recalls | "Come back in two weeks" is easy to say at the desk and easy to forget | Decides whom to chase when a recall is missed |
| The bill built from the visit | The services recorded in the consult are the services to charge for | Checks any discount and takes the payment |
| The day's numbers | Takings and appointments come from what the desk already recorded | Looks at them, and asks why when something looks wrong |

## What to keep with people

Some jobs should stay with a person even when software could do them.

- Clinical decisions. Software can draft a note or flag a possible missed diagnosis, but the doctor decides. In Cura AI every suggestion waits for the doctor to confirm it.
- Money going back out. Refunds and write-offs need a person who can say why.
- Replies to a patient who is unwell or upset. A patient who writes that they feel worse needs a reply from a person.
- Changes to a record after the visit. Anything that alters what was recorded should carry the name of the person who changed it.

For each job you do automate, write down the point where the software hands over, such as a patient replying to a reminder with a question, and who picks it up.

## Who is accountable when software acts

When a reminder goes to the wrong number or a bill goes out with the wrong fee, the patient calls the clinic, not the software company. In practice the clinic owns every message and every bill that goes out in its name, however it was produced.

So treat each automated job like a job you have given to a new member of staff:

1. Name the person who owns it. For reminders that is usually whoever runs the front desk, and for the bill it is whoever closes the day.
2. Check a sample. Read ten of the week's reminders, or compare the day's bills with the appointment list.
3. Know how to stop it. Before you switch a job on, find out how to switch it off, and who at the clinic has the login to do it.
4. Ask what the software records. A log of what was sent, and to whom, lets you answer a patient's complaint with facts.

Who is responsible for patient data in law depends on your country's data protection rules. For India, our guide to [patient data privacy laws in India](/patient-data-privacy-laws-in-india/) covers what the law asks of a clinic, including for data a software vendor handles on the clinic's behalf.

## Where automation stops helping

Automation moves a step off a person's desk. It does not fix a step that was wrong to begin with. If follow-ups are never booked at the desk, an automated recall has nothing to send. If the fee list is out of date, the bill built from the visit is wrong every time, only faster. Fix the manual step first, then automate it.

## How to start

1. Count one normal day: every job the desk repeats, and how many times.
2. Pick the job with the most repeats and the cheapest mistake. For most clinics that is reminders.
3. Name its owner, and the check they will make each week.
4. Run it for a month, compare the counts with the month before, and only then pick the next job.
