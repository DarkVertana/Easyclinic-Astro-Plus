If your clinic wants to see patients by video, the licence comes before the software. This post sets out what the Uganda Medical and Dental Practitioners Council (UMDPC) asks of a clinic that offers telemedicine, and what Uganda's data protection law and the Ministry of Health's guidelines say about the records. Each rule links to the document it comes from, as it stood on 26 September 2026. Rules change, so read the current version before you apply.

This post covers the UMDPC's rules only. If your clinic is licensed by another council, check that council's rules as well. It is a summary for clinic owners, not legal advice.

## What the UMDPC counts as telemedicine

The Council's [requirements for registration and licensure](https://umdpc.go.ug/downloads/requirements/requirements.pdf), in section 23 on digital health units, split digital health into two kinds of service:

- Telemedicine is real-time contact between a doctor and a patient for clinical care. The Council lists triage, diagnosis, treatment, e-prescription and remote patient monitoring.
- Telehealth covers the non-clinical services around care, such as patient education, communication between health workers, coordination of care, and sending images or lab results to a specialist for review.

Both need a licence. Telemedicine is subject to the same requirements as care in the clinic, and the Council expects care by video to be as safe and effective as care in person.

## Who can offer telemedicine in Uganda

Four rules in [section 23 of the Council's requirements](https://umdpc.go.ug/downloads/requirements/requirements.pdf) decide whether your clinic can run a telemedicine service at all:

- No person or body may run a digital health unit or service without a licence from the Council.
- A digital health service can only run as an adjunct to a private health unit the Council already licenses, and its licence is issued as an addition to that unit's licence. A video service with no licensed clinic behind it cannot be licensed.
- The parent facility must be licensed as a clinic by the Council, and equipped and supplied as a physical unit would be.
- Every practitioner offering the service must be registered and licensed by the Council, wherever they are, and employed by a licensed private health unit in Uganda.

The Council also says each digital health provider operates within a specific geographical area, and the licence lists every service the unit provides. The licences the parent clinic needs first are set out in our guide on [how to start a clinic in Uganda](/clinic-in-uganda/).

The last rule matters if you planned to bring in a specialist based abroad. Unless that specialist is licensed by the UMDPC and employed by a licensed unit in Uganda, they cannot offer telemedicine to your patients under these rules.

## Which patients you can see by video

The [Council's scope for telemedicine](https://umdpc.go.ug/downloads/requirements/requirements.pdf) says a video consult continues a clinical relationship with a doctor, or with another practitioner in the same practice, that began with an in-person consultation within the 12 months before the video consult. In practice:

- A patient's first consultation happens in the clinic.
- A patient seen in person in March can be followed up by video until the following March. After that, they come in again before the next video consult.
- A colleague in the same practice can hold the video consult for a patient another doctor saw in person.
- Keep the date of that in-person visit on the same file as the video consults, so anyone at the clinic can check the window.

Under these rules, video suits patients the clinic already knows, for reviewing a lab result or adjusting the dose for a patient on treatment for hypertension or diabetes. The doctor still decides whether a problem can be handled by video, and the Council holds that care to the in-person standard.

## What the Council checks, before and after licensing

For a digital health unit, [section 23](https://umdpc.go.ug/downloads/requirements/requirements.pdf) says the Council:

- Inspects the proposed unit on site, including its digital health infrastructure, policies, procedures and privacy notice. The policies must be in line with those of the parent clinic.
- Asks for a certificate of compliance showing that the unit's digital health platforms, software, policies and procedures meet the requirements of the Personal Data Protection Office (PDPO) at the National Information Technology Authority Uganda (NITA-U).
- Expects the unit to follow the Ministry of Health's digital health guidelines, which it lists by name, including the [Compendium of the National Digital Health Guidelines](https://library.health.go.ug/health-information-systems/digital-health/compendium-national-digital-health-guidelines) and the guidelines for electronic medical records systems.
- Reviews licensed units on the same inspection schedule as private health units, and its inspectors may examine telecommunication infrastructure and digital records.

The certificate of compliance is the step that involves your software, because it covers the platform you use. Ask your vendor early what it can give you towards it.

## What the data protection law says about the records

Uganda's [Data Protection and Privacy Act, 2019](https://bills.parliament.ug/attachments/Data%20Protection%20and%20Privacy%20Act%202019.pdf) applies to the record of every video consult, as it does to any clinic record.

- Section 7 makes the patient's prior consent the general rule for collecting or processing personal data, with exceptions that include medical purposes.
- Section 9 treats health status and medical records as special personal data. One of the grounds on which it allows them to be collected or processed is that the patient gives the information freely and with consent.
- Section 19 says a clinic that processes or stores personal data outside Uganda must make sure the other country protects it at least as well as the Act does, or that the patient has consented.
- Sections 5 and 29 set up a data protection register, kept by the PDPO, in which every person or institution collecting or processing personal data is registered.

The in-person visit that opens the 12-month window is a good moment to ask for consent to video follow-ups and to record the answer on the patient's file.

## What the Ministry of Health's EMR guidelines ask of private clinics

The Ministry's [Guidelines for the Implementation of the Electronic Medical Records System](https://library.health.go.ug/health-information-systems/digital-health/guidelines-implementation-electronic-medical-records) (January 2024) set requirements for private-sector EMR systems in section 4.3. Among them:

- The EMR system is certified by the Ministry of Health.
- It integrates with the national eHMIS/DHIS2 reporting system and produces at least the national HMIS reports.
- It and its health data are not hosted on a cloud outside Uganda without the approval of the PDPO and NITA-U.
- Patients can get their own records at no cost other than printing.

The same section says health data processed by the EMR is owned by the Government of Uganda and held in trust by the health facility. Private facilities show they comply through a self-assessment every quarter, with an external assessment by the Ministry once a year.

## Questions to put to any software vendor before you apply

- In which country is the data hosted? If it is outside Uganda, what does the vendor provide towards PDPO and NITA-U approval?
- What can the vendor give you for the PDPO certificate of compliance the Council asks for?
- Is the EMR certified by the Ministry of Health, and can it produce the HMIS reports for DHIS2?
- Does a video consult sit on the same patient record as clinic visits, so the in-person visit that opened the 12-month window is on screen?

Get the answers in writing, because the Council's inspection reviews the platform the unit runs on.
