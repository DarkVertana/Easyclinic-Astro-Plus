This post is for whoever keeps the stock book in a small clinic a long way from its supplier. In many rural clinics that is a nurse or a clinical officer who also sees patients, so the method has to be simple enough to keep up between consultations. It starts with the arithmetic and ends with the habits that keep the arithmetic honest.

## Why rural clinics run out

A stockout in a rural clinic seldom comes from one mistake. The supplier is often in a town a day's travel away, so an order can take weeks to arrive, and a late delivery gives no warning. The stock book is updated when someone has time, which on a busy day means after closing. Demand does not hold steady either. Where malaria rises with the rains, antimalarials go out faster in those months than any average suggests.

Put those together and the shortage is found at the counter, with a patient waiting. Every step below moves that moment earlier, to a day when there is still time to order.

## Set a reorder level for each item

A reorder level is the stock count at which you place the next order. Set it too low and the shelf empties before the delivery arrives. The rule is:

**Reorder level = average use per week × lead time in weeks + buffer stock**

Work out the average use from the last three months of dispensing records, not from memory. Leave out any week when the item was out of stock, because a week at zero makes demand look lower than it is.

Use the lead time you have actually had, from the day the order went out to the day the stock was on your shelf, including the trip to collect it. If the supplier says one week and your recent orders took up to four, plan on four. In Uganda, [National Medical Stores](https://nms.go.ug/about-us/) supplies public health facilities, and mission and private clinics can buy from wholesalers such as the [Joint Medical Store](https://jms.co.ug/about-us/), a private not-for-profit. Whichever supplier you use, time its deliveries yourself.

Here is the rule worked through for four items. The figures are an example of the arithmetic, not a recommendation for any medicine or clinic.

| Item | Average use a week | Lead time | Buffer stock | Reorder level |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Antimalarial, adult packs | 10 packs | 3 weeks | 20 packs | 50 packs |
| Amoxicillin | 6 packs | 3 weeks | 12 packs | 30 packs |
| Oral rehydration salts | 40 sachets | 2 weeks | 80 sachets | 160 sachets |
| Malaria rapid tests | 15 tests | 3 weeks | 45 tests | 90 tests |

Set a maximum for each item as well: the reorder level plus what you use in the weeks each order should last. When the count falls to the reorder level, order the maximum minus what is on the shelf. For the antimalarial above, with orders meant to last four weeks, the maximum is 90 packs, so an order placed at 50 packs is for 40.

## Keep a buffer for the medicines you cannot be without

Buffer stock covers a late delivery or a busy month. It also ties up money, so do not hold the same buffer for everything. Make a short list of the items the clinic cannot run out of: the medicines a patient cannot wait for, such as an antimalarial for a child with a fever, and the tests that decide whether to give them. Your doctors should agree the list, and it should fit on one page.

Give the items on that list a larger buffer and check them every week. For everything else, a smaller buffer and a monthly check are enough. When an item on the short list falls to its reorder level, the order goes out that day, not at the end of the week.

## Plan for the rains and other surges

A reorder level worked out from an average month will fail in the busiest month. Look at last year's dispensing records month by month for the items on your short list, and mark the months when use climbed. Where malaria rises with the rains, antimalarials and rapid tests climb together.

Raise the reorder levels for those items at least one lead time before the season starts, so the extra stock arrives before the patients do. When the season ends, lower them again, or the extra stock sits on the shelf until it expires.

A surge can lengthen the lead time too. When every clinic in the district orders the same medicine in the same fortnight, the supplier can run short as well. Order early rather than ordering more at the last minute.

## Dispense the batch that expires first

FEFO means first expiry, first out: the pack with the nearest expiry date is dispensed first, whichever delivery it came in. Most of the time that is the oldest delivery. When a new delivery brings a batch with a shorter date than the stock already on the shelf, FEFO sends the new batch out first, where FIFO (first in, first out) would leave it to expire.

On the shelf, put the nearest dates at the front. Once a month, list the items that expire within the next three months and use them first, or move them to a clinic that will. If you plan to return short-dated stock, ask the supplier whether it takes returns before you rely on it.

Ordering far more than you use is not a cure for stockouts. Medicines have dates, and an order that expires on the shelf is money that could have paid for the item that ran out.

## Count the stock on a fixed day

A stock book is only as good as its last count. Count the short-list items every week, on the same day, and count everything once a month. Compare each count with the book. When they disagree, find out why that week: a dispensing that was never written down, a damaged pack, a transfer nobody recorded, or stock that was taken. A difference found a month later has no explanation left.

## When the power or the internet goes down

If you keep stock in software, plan for the hours when it is out of reach. Easy Clinic is cloud software and does not run fully offline today, so a clinic on an unreliable line needs a backup connection, such as a mobile hotspot, and a paper tally sheet at the counter. Enter what was dispensed on paper as soon as the connection is back. Until you do, the count on the screen is higher than the count on the shelf.

## What Easy Clinic's pharmacy module does

Software does not shorten a lead time or fix a supplier, but it can keep the count without anyone updating a book. In Easy Clinic's pharmacy and inventory module:

- The pharmacist dispenses from the doctor's prescription on screen, and the stock count falls as each medicine is dispensed.
- Each item carries a reorder level. The software suggests the level and an order quantity from past use and lead times, and an alert goes out when stock falls below it.
- Each batch is tracked with its expiry date. The counter is warned when a batch is near expiry, and the batch nearest its date is put forward to dispense first.
- An adjustment for a shortfall or a damaged pack needs authorisation, and every stock transaction goes into an audit log.

The module is added to the Professional or Premium plan, or comes with Enterprise. Its suggestions are only as good as the use and lead-time figures behind them, and the clinic still decides what to stock. Where one organisation runs several rural clinics, a shortage at one clinic can sit beside a surplus at another.
