Four messages can reach a clinic's WhatsApp in the same minute. One asks to move tomorrow's appointment. One asks what time the clinic opens on Sunday. One is a photo of a blood report with the words "Please check". One says a father has chest tightness and is sweating, and asks whether they can come now.

On the phone they arrive with the same sound and sit in the same list, and a busy desk tends to answer them in the order they came. This post sets out a simple way for the person at the desk to sort them instead, during the day and after the clinic closes.

## Why the desk cannot answer in the order messages arrive

In an emergency department, triage is the assessment a doctor or nurse makes of each patient to decide the order they are seen, so the most seriously unwell are seen first, as the [NHS explains](https://www.nhs.uk/nhs-services/urgent-and-emergency-care-services/when-to-go-to-ae/). On a clinic's WhatsApp, the person reading the messages is often the receptionist, who is not trained to assess anyone.

So the desk's job is narrower than a nurse's. It is to spot the message that might be an emergency and get it to a clinician at once. Everything else goes where the right person will answer it. The receptionist never decides that a symptom is minor.

## Two questions for every message

Every message gets the same two questions, in this order:

1. Does it describe a symptom or a result, including a problem after treatment at the clinic? A photo of a report or a wound with "Please check" counts.
2. If it does, is it on the doctor's red-flag list?

The answer decides where the message goes.

| What the message is about | Where it goes | Who answers | When |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Anything on the red-flag list | Emergency reply now, then a phone call to the doctor or nurse on duty | The desk sends the reply, the clinician calls back | Straight away |
| Any other symptom, or a question about medicines | Clinical queue | The doctor or nurse | By the time the doctor has set, such as the end of the session |
| A report or result: "Is this normal?" | Reports queue | The doctor | By the time given in the acknowledgement |
| A new booking, or a change to one | Bookings queue | The desk | The same session |
| Fees, timings, directions or a bill | General queue | The desk, with a saved reply | The same session |

If the desk is unsure whether a message is clinical, it is clinical. A wrong guess in that direction costs the doctor a moment. A wrong guess the other way can cost a patient much more.

If the clinic's WhatsApp runs on the [WhatsApp Business app](https://whatsappbusiness.com/products/business-app-features/), its labels can hold the queues: give each row of the table a label, and the desk can filter the chats to see one queue at a time.

## Write the red-flag list with your doctor

The red-flag list belongs to the doctor, not the desk. Ask the doctor to write it for your clinic's patients and pin it next to the phone that holds the clinic's WhatsApp. A general practice might start with chest pain or tightness, difficulty breathing, heavy bleeding, any sudden change for the worse, and a problem after a procedure the clinic did. The doctor adds the rest, and a specialist adds the warning signs for their own patients.

Keep the list short enough to read in one glance. When a message slips past it, add the words the patient actually used, because patients do not always write "chest pain". They may write "my chest feels heavy" or "pressure since morning".

## The replies to write before you need them

The WhatsApp Business app also lets the desk save quick replies for messages it sends often, and any clinic can keep the same replies in a note beside the phone. Write these with the doctor and send them as written. The desk does not add advice to any of them.

- **The emergency reply.** "If this is an emergency, such as chest pain or difficulty breathing, please call an ambulance or go to the nearest emergency department now. Do not wait for a reply on WhatsApp. We have passed your message to the doctor."
- **A symptom for the clinical queue.** "Thank you. We have passed your message to the doctor, who will reply by 5 pm today. If it gets worse before then, go to the nearest emergency department."
- **A report or result.** "Thank you, we have your report. The doctor will look at it and we will reply by tomorrow evening."
- **A new booking.** "To book, please send in one message the patient's name, the doctor you want to see, the reason for the visit and the day that suits you." Asking for everything at once saves a round of questions for each missing detail.
- **Timings and directions.** One saved reply with the clinic's opening hours and a map link.

The times in these samples are examples. Put in the times your doctors can keep, and change the reply when a doctor is on leave.

## After the clinic closes

Messages keep arriving after the shutters come down. The [WhatsApp Business app](https://whatsappbusiness.com/products/business-app-features/) can send an away message automatically in reply to messages that come in outside your business hours, and a clinic on other software should set up the same reply there. That message has two jobs: tell a patient with an emergency what to do, and say honestly when the clinic will read the message.

A good after-hours message reads something like this: "The clinic is closed and messages are read from 9 am. If this is an emergency, call an ambulance or go to the nearest emergency department now." If one of your doctors does watch the WhatsApp at night, the message can say so and until what time. If nobody does, do not imply that someone will.

The next morning, whoever opens the WhatsApp reads the overnight messages with the two questions before touching the bookings. A symptom that arrived at 11 pm is still a symptom at 9 am, and the doctor should see it before the first patient is called in.

## Keep the triage on the clinic's own number

Run the WhatsApp on a number and a phone that belong to the clinic, not on a receptionist's own handset. When a person leaves, the chats and the patients' numbers leave with them, and so does any trace of which symptom message went to which doctor.

Name one person per session who watches the WhatsApp, and one doctor or nurse they pass clinical messages to. For a red flag, the desk phones that clinician after sending the emergency reply. A forwarded screenshot can sit unread in a doctor's chats between consults.

Check the rules where you practise before a doctor gives advice by chat. WhatsApp's own [Business Messaging Policy](https://whatsappbusiness.com/policy/) says not to use WhatsApp for telemedicine or to send or request health information where the regulations that apply forbid sending it to systems that do not meet heightened requirements.

Put the clinic's number in any automated reply, whichever kind of WhatsApp the clinic runs.

## How to tell whether it is working

Once a week, sit down with the doctor and the person who watches the WhatsApp and look at every red-flag message from the week. For each one, note when it arrived, when the desk saw it, when the emergency reply went and when a clinician called back. Then look for the messages that should have been flagged and were not, and add their words to the list.

Two more numbers are worth writing down each evening: the chats still unanswered at closing time, and the replies that went out later than the time the clinic promised. If either keeps growing, the clinic has more WhatsApp than one person per session can handle, and it is time to move some of the outgoing messages to the software or share the watch.
