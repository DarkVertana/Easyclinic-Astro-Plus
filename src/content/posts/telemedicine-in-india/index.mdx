The simplest way to start a telemedicine practice in India is to offer some of the follow-ups you already see in the clinic by video, and add more once the routine works. This post covers which consultations suit video, what India's telemedicine guidelines ask of the doctor, what to set up before the first call and how video consults run on EasyClinic.

## Which consultations suit video

The [Telemedicine Practice Guidelines](https://esanjeevani.mohfw.gov.in/assets/guidelines/Telemedicine_Practice_Guidelines.pdf) leave the choice to the doctor: the registered medical practitioner decides whether a teleconsult is enough or the patient needs to be seen in person. The same guidelines note that a teleconsult cannot replace an examination that needs touch, such as palpation or auscultation.

In practice, video suits the consultations where the history and the reports carry most of the decision:

- a review of blood pressure or blood sugar readings the patient has taken at home
- going through lab or scan reports with the patient
- a medicine review for a condition you already treat
- advice on diet and exercise, or counselling

It also saves the journey for patients who travel a long way for a short review. A new complaint that needs examining belongs in the clinic. For emergencies, the [guidelines](https://esanjeevani.mohfw.gov.in/assets/guidelines/Telemedicine_Practice_Guidelines.pdf) say telemedicine should be avoided where in-person care is available, and limited to first aid, life-saving measures, counselling and advice on referral, with the patient told to see a doctor in person as soon as possible.

## Start with follow-ups, and keep the first visit in person

The [guidelines](https://esanjeevani.mohfw.gov.in/assets/guidelines/Telemedicine_Practice_Guidelines.pdf) sort teleconsults into first consults and follow-ups. A follow-up is a consult with the same doctor, within six months of the previous in-person consult, for the same condition. A gap of more than six months, or new symptoms outside the condition you were treating, makes it a first consult.

The difference matters for prescribing. Under the [guidelines](https://esanjeevani.mohfw.gov.in/assets/guidelines/Telemedicine_Practice_Guidelines.pdf), List O medicines, such as paracetamol and ORS, can be prescribed on any teleconsult. List A medicines can be prescribed on a first consult only if it is held by video, and re-prescribed as a refill at a follow-up. List B medicines can be added only at a follow-up, on top of what was prescribed in person for the same condition. Medicines in Schedule X, and narcotic and psychotropic substances under the NDPS Act, cannot be prescribed on a teleconsult at all. The [telehealth page](/features/telehealth/#nmc-guidelines) sets the lists out as a table.

That points to a hybrid routine. See the patient in person first, where you examine and prescribe. Hold the follow-ups for the same condition by video over the next six months, and bring the patient back in when something changes or six months have passed. If a first consult has to be remote, hold it by video rather than by phone, because under the [guidelines](https://esanjeevani.mohfw.gov.in/assets/guidelines/Telemedicine_Practice_Guidelines.pdf) a first consult by audio or text allows only List O medicines.

## What the guidelines ask on every teleconsult

The [Telemedicine Practice Guidelines](https://esanjeevani.mohfw.gov.in/assets/guidelines/Telemedicine_Practice_Guidelines.pdf) set out steps for every teleconsult. These are the ones that change how a clinic works:

- **Say who you are.** The doctor starts by giving their name and qualifications, and shows their registration number on prescriptions, the clinic website, messages to patients and receipts.
- **Confirm who the patient is.** The doctor confirms the patient's identity from details such as name and age, and asks the patient's age before prescribing. A minor can consult only together with an adult whose identity is also confirmed.
- **Consent.** If the patient starts the teleconsult, consent is implied. If anyone else starts it, such as the doctor or a health worker, the patient's explicit consent is needed. The patient can give it in a message or on the call, and the doctor records it.
- **The prescription.** The doctor gives the patient a scanned or digital copy of the signed prescription, or an e-prescription, by email or any messaging platform. Sending it straight to a pharmacy needs the patient's explicit consent to have the medicines dispensed at a pharmacy of their choice.
- **Records.** Keep a log of the teleconsult, such as the call or chat record, with the reports used in it, and keep prescription records as you would for a clinic visit.
- **Fees.** A teleconsult is treated like a clinic visit for fees, and the patient gets a receipt or invoice.

Following these rules is the doctor's responsibility, whatever software runs the call. This post follows the 2020 text as the Health Ministry publishes it on its eSanjeevani site, so check with your State Medical Council or the National Medical Commission for any later change before you rely on it.

## What to set up before the first video consult

1. **Choose the consults and the times.** For example, blood pressure and diabetes follow-ups on two afternoons a week, so video patients do not wait behind the clinic queue.
2. **Set the fee and collect it before the call.** A paid video follow-up replaces free advice over the phone, and a fee collected at booking does not have to be chased.
3. **Get the room right.** A quiet room, a camera at eye level, a headset and a connection you have tested, with mobile data on a spare phone as a backup.
4. **Decide who books and who joins.** The desk books video slots like clinic visits and tells the patient how to join. The doctor reads the patient's history before the call.
5. **Tell your patients.** Mention it at the end of a clinic visit, and in the reminder for a follow-up, so patients know the next review can be by video.
6. **Look back after a month.** Count the video consults, the ones that needed an in-person visit afterwards, the patients who paid before the call and the follow-ups booked from them.
