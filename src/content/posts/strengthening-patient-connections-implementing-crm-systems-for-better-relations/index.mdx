A clinic without CRM software still does CRM work. It is the receptionist who remembers which patients are due back, and the notebook of phone numbers they ring when the appointment book looks thin. CRM stands for customer relationship management, and CRM software puts that memory into a system, so the work carries on when the receptionist is on leave. This post explains what a clinic CRM does beside the EMR, and how to tell whether your clinic software already does the same jobs.

## What a clinic CRM is, and how it differs from the EMR

The EMR is the clinical record: what the doctor found and what was prescribed. A CRM is the contact record. It holds what the clinic sent each patient and when, whether the patient replied, when they are due back and what they said about their last visit.

The two meet at the appointment book. A reminder needs the appointment, and a recall for a diabetes review needs the diagnosis from the chart. A CRM kept apart from the EMR therefore needs someone, or some software, to keep the two in step. That is why some clinic software carries the contact jobs on the same patient record as the clinical ones.

## The jobs a CRM does in a clinic

| Job | What it looks like at the desk | What it needs to know |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reminder before the visit | A message the day before, and a short one on the day | The appointment time and the doctor |
| Recall when a review is due | A list of patients whose three-month review has passed without a booking | The diagnosis and the date of the last visit |
| Registration before the first visit | A form link the patient fills in at home, instead of a clipboard in the waiting room | Where on the patient record the answers go |
| Feedback after the visit | A short survey asking how the visit went | Which visit, and which doctor |
| Follow-up on an enquiry | A call back to someone who asked about a treatment and has not booked | Nothing yet, because the person is not a patient |
| Contact history | Who was messaged, and whether they replied | The patient's phone number and the messages sent |

Only the enquiry row sits outside the patient record. The others start from something the clinic software already holds, such as an appointment or a diagnosis. That is the question to settle before you buy anything: does your current software already do the jobs that start from the patient record?

## Does your clinic software already do this?

Put these questions to your current software, or to its support team:

- Does it send reminders on its own, at a time you set before each visit?
- Can you see which patients acknowledged a reminder, so the desk calls only the rest?
- Can it list the patients due for a review from what is recorded on their chart?
- Can a new patient fill in the registration form on their phone before arriving?
- Does it ask patients about the visit afterwards, and keep the answers?
- Can the front desk see contact details and appointments without opening the clinical notes?

If most of the answers are yes, a separate CRM will mostly repeat what you have. If most are no, check whether the features exist but were never switched on before you buy a second system.

## When a separate CRM is worth it

A dedicated CRM earns its upkeep in a few situations:

- You follow up many enquiries from people who have not booked yet, for treatments where the decision takes weeks, such as a dental implant or an IVF cycle.
- You run a central call centre that books for several branches and needs one queue of calls to make.
- You market to people who are not on your patient list at all.
- Your clinic software cannot send messages, and changing it is not possible this year.

A second system has a cost beyond its price. Somebody has to keep the patient list in step between the two, and each patient's phone number, and often their condition, now sits in two places with two sets of logins to control.

## Setting it up without swamping the front desk

1. Pick one problem first. If patients miss appointments, start with reminders. If reviews slip, start with recalls. Write down this month's number before you switch anything on.
2. Write the messages in the clinic's own words. Keep them short, with the doctor's name and the clinic's phone number.
3. Train the desk on that one job, and give it a month.
4. Add the next job once the first runs without anyone chasing it.
5. Compare the numbers with the month before you started.

## Patient data and consent

Messages use a patient's phone number, and a recall by condition uses their diagnosis as well. Whether you need the patient's consent before sending them, and how a patient stops them, depends on the data protection law in your country. The [consent table on the patient engagement page](/features/patient-engagement/#consent-by-country) sets out what the laws of India, Kenya, the UAE and Nigeria ask, in general terms.

Whatever system you use, give the front desk the contact details and the appointment book, and keep the clinical notes to the clinical staff.

## What to measure

- No-shows each week, counted from the appointment book.
- Reviews that fell due, against reviews that were booked.
- Replies to feedback surveys, and what they say about waiting times.
- Enquiries that became a first booking, if you track enquiries.

Measure against your own clinic's numbers from before you started. We have no measured figure from EasyClinic clinics to give you, and a number from another clinic would tell you little about yours.
