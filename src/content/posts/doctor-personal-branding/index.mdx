Someone on the team will ask, sooner or later, whether the doctor should be posting short videos. It is a fair question with a rules half and a practical half. This post takes the rules first, because in India they decide what kind of video a doctor can make at all.

## What the 2002 regulations say about publicity

Registered medical practitioners in India are bound by the [Indian Medical Council (Professional Conduct, Etiquette and Ethics) Regulations, 2002](https://www.nmc.org.in/page/rules-regulations-rules-regulations-of-erstwhile-mci-code-of-medical-ethics-regulations-2002). The National Medical Commission [issued new conduct regulations on 2 August 2023](https://www.nmc.org.in/page/rules-regulations-rules-regulations-nmc), then [held them in abeyance on 23 August 2023](https://www.nmc.org.in/storage/cms/rules-regulation-nmc/KEpwkMzgUwYNR49OrJaqvLcIpeytGPg7XGcorhTg.pdf) and made the 2002 regulations effective again. These are the clauses a doctor's videos run into:

- **Publicity about yourself.** Regulation 6.1.1 calls soliciting patients, directly or indirectly, unethical. A doctor must not be the subject of advertising or publicity that invites attention to their position, skill, qualifications, achievements, specialities or honours, or that would amount to self-aggrandizement.
- **Boasting of results.** The same regulation forbids a doctor to boast of cases, operations, cures or remedies, or to permit a report of them to be published.
- **Endorsements.** It also forbids a doctor to endorse or recommend any drug, medicine, appliance or commercial product for use with their name or photograph in advertising, paid or unpaid.
- **Writing and talks.** Regulation 7.11 says a doctor should not write articles or give interviews in the lay press on diseases and treatments that have the effect of advertising the doctor. It allows writing under the doctor's own name on public health and hygienic living, and broadcast or internet talks for the same purpose.
- **Degrees after your name.** Regulation 1.4.2 allows as suffixes to a doctor's name only recognised medical degrees, and certificates, diplomas, memberships or honours that confer professional knowledge or recognise an exemplary qualification or achievement.
- **Patients.** Regulation 7.17 forbids publishing photographs or case reports that identify a patient without their permission, and says no consent is needed when the identity is not disclosed. Regulation 7.14 forbids disclosing a patient's secrets learnt in practice, except in listed cases such as a court order.

What regulation 6.1.1 does allow is narrow: a formal press announcement on occasions such as starting practice or changing address, and a public declaration of charges. A complaint of misconduct goes to the appropriate Medical Council, which under regulation 8.2 holds an enquiry and can remove a doctor's name from the register for a period or altogether.

This post follows the text on the National Medical Commission's site on 26 September 2026. Check with your State Medical Council before you rely on it, and note that dentists and AYUSH practitioners answer to their own councils' codes, which this post does not cover.

## Where that leaves a doctor on Reels and Shorts

The regulations were written before Reels and Shorts. The nearest they come is regulation 7.11, which allows broadcast and internet talks on public health and hygienic living, and warns against articles and interviews on diseases and treatments that advertise the doctor. Read for short video, as this post reads them (a State Medical Council may read them differently), they split what a doctor might post into two groups.

Sits more comfortably, because it informs rather than promotes:

- public health and healthy habits, such as vaccination or screening
- when a symptom needs a doctor today
- how to prepare for a test or a procedure
- a common myth, answered with the evidence

Runs into regulation 6.1.1 or 7.11:

- "best doctor in the city", awards or patient counts
- before-and-after results, or a patient's cure told as a story
- a medicine, supplement, device or other product shown or named with approval
- your specialities and qualifications presented as the reason to choose you
- a treatment you offer, presented so that viewers book it with you

The line between the two groups is the one the regulations draw: whether the video invites attention to the doctor or to the subject. The risk comes when the video is about you.

## Patients in the comments

Short videos can bring questions, and some are personal: a commenter describes their symptoms and asks what to take. Do not answer it in public. Reply with a general line, such as "This needs an examination, please book a consultation", and never confirm that a commenter is your patient. Under regulation 7.14, a comment thread is no place for anything learnt in a consultation.

Decide who reads the comments and the direct messages, and move anything personal to the clinic's phone or WhatsApp number. End each video by saying it is general information, not a diagnosis.

## Video ideas that inform

The best material is the question your desk already answers many times a day. Keep each video, and its caption, about the subject rather than the doctor.

- **The preparation question.** What to eat before an ultrasound, or which documents to bring. One clear video, pinned to the profile, can be sent to every patient who asks.
- **What a follow-up visit is for.** Why the review matters even when the patient feels fine.
- **When to worry.** When a child's fever needs a doctor tonight and when it can wait for the morning.
- **Clinic policy.** How late arrivals and rescheduling work, explained calmly before anyone is upset at the counter.

Record them on a fixed day. Before each one goes out, run the same checklist: no identifiable patient without written permission, no claim of results, no product or brand, a source for any medical fact, and the general-information line at the end. The checklist matters more than the camera.

## When a video brings calls

If a video does bring calls and messages, the front desk takes them on top of the day's patients. Two things help. Keep booked slots and walk-ins on one calendar, so the desk can offer a real time without putting the caller on hold. And send each new booking a reminder before the visit.
