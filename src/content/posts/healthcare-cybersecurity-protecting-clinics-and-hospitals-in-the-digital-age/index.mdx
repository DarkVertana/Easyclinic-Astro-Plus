If your clinic has no IT staff, this checklist is for you. The desk computer holds the appointment book and the scanned reports, and its password may be known only to whoever set it up years ago. This post starts with what an attack looks like at the desk and ends with what the law in India and Kenya expects once something goes wrong. It is not legal advice, and a lawyer should check which duties apply to your clinic.

## What an attack on a small clinic looks like

**Ransomware.** A program gets onto one computer, for example through an email attachment, and locks the files on it and on any shared drive it can reach, with a demand for payment to release them. If the appointment book or the records are on that computer, the desk stops. Under India's DPDP Act, a personal data breach includes any unauthorised processing of personal data that compromises its availability, among other things ([section 2(u)](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2024/06/2bf1f0e9f04e6fb4f8fef35e82c42aa5.pdf)), so records locked by ransomware can count as a breach even if nothing was copied.

**Phishing.** An email or WhatsApp message that looks as if it comes from a bank or from the clinic's software company asks someone to log in through a link or open an attachment. The link leads to a fake login page, and the password typed there opens the real system to whoever sent it.

**Shared and forgotten logins.** One password for the whole desk, written on a note beside the screen, and the login of a receptionist who left last year that still works. When everyone uses the same login, nobody can tell who opened a record.

**Lost phones and laptops.** A phone with photos of lab reports in its gallery, or a laptop with the patient list in a spreadsheet, left in a taxi.

## Give every person their own login

- One login per person, never shared, in the clinic software and in email.
- Each role opens only what it needs. The front desk needs the appointment book and the bill, and it does not need the doctor's notes.
- On a leaver's last day, switch off their login and change any password they knew, including the Wi-Fi.
- Turn on two-step login, a code on the phone as well as the password, for email and for any system that offers it.
- Keep passwords out of sight. A password manager is better than a notebook, and a notebook in a locked drawer is better than a note on the screen.

India's [DPDP Rules 2025](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/11/53450e6e5dc0bfa85ebd78686cadad39.pdf) list, among the minimum safeguards in rule 6, measures to control access to computer resources and logs that show who accessed personal data, so that unauthorised access can be detected and investigated. Rule 6 applies from May 2027.

## Keep devices and software up to date

- Switch on automatic updates for the operating system and the web browser on every clinic computer and phone.
- Replace a computer whose operating system no longer gets security updates, because the holes that updates would have closed stay open.
- Set a screen lock on every device that opens patient records, and lock the desk computer when the receptionist steps away.
- Give patients their own Wi-Fi, separate from the network the desk computer uses.
- Keep antivirus switched on and updated on the clinic's Windows computers.

## Back up, and test the restore

Until you have restored a file from a backup, you do not know that the backup works.

If your records are in cloud clinic software, the vendor backs up that data, so ask how often and how a restore works (the questions are at the end of this post). The files on your own computers are your job: scanned reports, the accounts spreadsheet, letters and consent forms. Back them up on a schedule, and keep one copy that is not connected to the clinic network, such as a drive you unplug after each backup, so ransomware on the network cannot reach it.

Once a month, restore one file from the backup and open it.

Both countries' laws point the same way. Rule 6 of India's [DPDP Rules 2025](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/11/53450e6e5dc0bfa85ebd78686cadad39.pdf) names data-backups as a way to keep processing if data is destroyed or access is lost. Kenya's [Data Protection Act](https://www.odpc.go.ke/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/TheDataProtectionAct__No24of2019.pdf) lists, among the measures a clinic should consider in section 41(4), encryption and the ability to restore the availability of personal data in a timely manner after a physical or technical incident.

## Teach the desk to spot phishing

Four signs to look for in any message:

- It is urgent: an account about to close, a payment about to bounce.
- It asks you to log in through a link.
- It carries an attachment nobody was expecting.
- It asks for a change of bank details or a payment to a new account.

Two rules follow. Type the web address yourself, or use a saved bookmark, instead of clicking a link to a login page. If a supplier or the bank asks for something unusual, call them on a number you already have, not the one in the message.

Tell staff, too, that anyone who clicked should say so straight away. A password changed within minutes does less damage than one found out next week.

## Keep patient reports off personal phones

Reports sent from a receptionist's own WhatsApp stay in that person's chats and gallery after they leave the clinic, and go with the phone if it is lost. Send reports from a number or a system the clinic controls, and ask staff to delete photos of reports from their own phones. Kenya's [Data Protection Act](https://www.odpc.go.ke/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/TheDataProtectionAct__No24of2019.pdf) lists health status among sensitive personal data in section 2, which is a good reason to treat these photos with care anywhere.

## Write the incident plan before you need it

Write one page and print it for the desk:

1. Who decides, and whom they call first: your IT help and your software vendor, with both numbers on the page.
2. How the clinic keeps seeing patients: a paper register and the day's appointment list, printed each morning.
3. What gets written down as it happens: what you found, when you found it, which records were involved and what you did.
4. Who tells patients and the regulator, and by when, from the rules below.

### In India

[Section 8(6) of the DPDP Act](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2024/06/2bf1f0e9f04e6fb4f8fef35e82c42aa5.pdf) says a Data Fiduciary must tell the Data Protection Board and each affected patient about a personal data breach. A Data Fiduciary is whoever determines the purpose and means of processing personal data ([section 2(i)](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2024/06/2bf1f0e9f04e6fb4f8fef35e82c42aa5.pdf)), and a clinic deciding how its own patient records are used is one. [Rule 7 of the DPDP Rules 2025](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/11/53450e6e5dc0bfa85ebd78686cadad39.pdf) sets out how. Each affected patient is told without delay, in plain language, what happened, what it may mean for them, what the clinic is doing about it, what they can do to protect themselves and whom to contact. The Board gets a description without delay and a detailed report within 72 hours of the clinic becoming aware, unless the Board allows longer on a written request.

Neither of these two duties applies yet. Rule 7 comes into force in May 2027, 18 months after the Rules were published on 13 November 2025 (rule 1(4)), and section 8 of the Act comes into force on the same date under the government's [commencement notification, G.S.R. 843(E)](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/11/c56ceae6c383460ca69577428d36828b.pdf), part of the 18-month phased timeline in the [government's release](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2190014) on the Rules. Other Indian rules on reporting cyber incidents may apply sooner, and this post does not cover them, so ask your lawyer. Write the plan now either way. The rest of the privacy duties for Indian clinics are in the guide to [patient data privacy laws in India](/patient-data-privacy-laws-in-india/).

### In Kenya

Under [section 43 of the Data Protection Act 2019](https://www.odpc.go.ke/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/TheDataProtectionAct__No24of2019.pdf), when personal data has been accessed or acquired by an unauthorised person and there is a real risk of harm to the patient, the clinic as data controller notifies the Data Commissioner without delay and within 72 hours of becoming aware. A notification that comes later must give the reasons for the delay.

The affected patients are told in writing within a reasonably practical period, with enough detail to protect themselves, though section 43(6) does not require this where the clinic had safeguards such as encryption on the affected data. The clinic also keeps a written record of the breach and of the remedial action taken (section 43(8)). If your software vendor processes the data for you and finds the breach, section 43(3) asks it to tell you without delay, and within 48 hours where reasonably practicable, so put the vendor's breach contact on your one-page plan.

If your clinic is in another country, its own data protection law sets the rules. This post covers India and Kenya only.

## Questions to ask your clinic software vendor

- Where is our data hosted, and who at your company can see it?
- How often do you back up our data, and how long does a restore take?
- Can each member of staff have their own login that opens only what their role needs?
- Is there a record of who opened or changed a patient's record, and can we see it?
- How and how fast will you tell us about a breach on your side?
- Can we export all our data if we leave, and in what format?

In Kenya, [section 42(2) of the Data Protection Act](https://www.odpc.go.ke/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/TheDataProtectionAct__No24of2019.pdf) asks the clinic to choose a processor that gives sufficient guarantees and to sign a written contract that binds it to act only on the clinic's instructions, so these answers belong in writing.
