A patient can have a good consult and still leave annoyed about the hour on the bench before it. The good news for a clinic owner is that the wait can be measured with a pen and a sheet of paper, and several of its usual causes sit in how the day is booked. This post sets out how to find where your clinic's wait comes from, and the fix that matches each cause.

## Measure the wait before you change anything

For two weeks, write down four times for every patient: when they check in at the desk, when they go in to the doctor, when they come out, and when they leave the clinic after paying or collecting medicines. A printed sheet at the desk is enough. Note beside each patient whether it was a new visit or a follow-up, and whether they booked or walked in.

At the end of the two weeks, the sheet shows how long each patient waited before seeing the doctor and how long they waited afterwards. Look for four patterns:

- The hour when the queue starts to build, and whether it is the same every day.
- The visit types that run over the time they were booked for.
- The doctor whose first patient goes in late.
- When walk-ins arrive, and how many there are on each day of the week.

Ask a few patients each week how long they felt they waited and whether anyone told them why. The answers tell you whether the problem is the wait itself or the silence around it.

## Set slot lengths from how long visits really take

A clinic that books every patient into a 10-minute slot falls behind as soon as a new patient arrives who needs 20 minutes. Each such visit adds 10 minutes to the wait of everyone booked after it, and by late morning the delay has stacked up.

Use your two weeks of times to set a length for each visit type: the time a follow-up usually takes, and the time a new patient usually takes. Book each visit type into its own length. If a procedure or a long counselling visit runs well over, give it its own slot type as well.

Then leave a short gap in the book every hour or two. A gap lets the doctor catch up after a visit that overran, instead of carrying the delay to the end of the session.

## Put walk-ins and bookings on one list, with a rule for who goes next

The worst waiting rooms are the ones where nobody knows the order. A patient with a 10:15 booking sits beside four walk-ins who came at nine, and the receptionist settles it by memory and by whoever complains loudest.

Pick a rule and tell patients what it is. One simple rule: booked patients go in at their time, and walk-ins fill the gaps between them. Your two weeks of data show when walk-ins arrive, so you can keep a few slots open in those hours instead of squeezing walk-ins into a full book.

Add one exception: a patient who needs to be seen sooner goes first, and the desk says so to the room. Saying why stops the room from assuming that someone jumped the queue.

In EasyClinic, walk-ins join the same calendar as booked appointments, and the desk can move a patient up the queue by need or by appointment type, so the order is on the screen rather than in one person's head.

## Start the first consult on time

The first appointment sets the pace for the whole session. If the doctor starts 30 minutes late, every patient booked that morning waits 30 minutes longer, unless a gap later in the book absorbs it.

If your times show that the doctor reliably arrives after a hospital round, book the first patient for when the doctor actually arrives, not for when the clinic opens. A book that matches the doctor's real day produces a shorter wait than one that matches the sign on the door.

## Refill cancelled slots from a waitlist

When a booked patient cancels, the slot can go to someone who would otherwise walk in at the busiest hour, or who is booked weeks away. Keep a list of patients who asked for an earlier appointment, and when a slot frees up, the desk calls down the list. Keep the list wherever the desk will actually look at it; a sheet by the phone works.

Reminders help because they tell you earlier which slots will be free. EasyClinic sends reminders by SMS and email on every plan, and on WhatsApp as well on Premium, and it tracks which patients have acknowledged theirs, so the desk calls only the patients who have not. EasyClinic has not published a measured figure for how much its reminders cut no-shows, and this post does not claim one.

## Tell waiting patients how long they will wait

A patient told that the doctor is running 40 minutes late can step out for a cup of tea or move the appointment. A patient told nothing queues at the counter to ask, and the receptionist loses time answering the same question.

Say it out loud when the doctor is late, and say roughly by how much. In EasyClinic, waiting patients are also told automatically when their wait changes, so the update does not depend on the receptionist finding a quiet moment.

## Shorten the wait after the consult

The wait after the consult counts as much to the patient as the wait before it. If your times show patients standing at the counter to pay, look at how the bill is raised. In EasyClinic the bill and the payment are done from the appointment screen in one click when the consult ends, so the desk is not writing up the bill from memory while the next patient waits.

If the queue after the consult is at the pharmacy counter, the fix is in how prescriptions reach it, which the [pharmacy and inventory page](/features/pharmacy-and-inventory/) covers.

## Check the numbers every month

Repeat the two-week measurement after each change, and change one thing at a time, so you know which change worked. Look at the same four patterns each time: when the queue builds, which visit types overrun, whether the first consult starts on time, and when walk-ins arrive.

Be honest about the limit. If the clinic books more patients than the doctor's hours can hold, no rule in the appointment book removes the wait. The fix then is another session in the busy hours or another doctor, and your measurements will show which hours need them.
