A clinic owner already has most of the data behind these numbers, in the appointment book and the bills. A KPI turns one of those records into a number you can compare from one month to the next. The hard part is choosing, because every number you track needs someone with time to act on it.

## Choose the KPIs that match a problem you have

Start from what is going wrong, not from a list. For example:

- Patients complain about the wait. Track the average wait time.
- The waiting room is full but the takings are flat. Track revenue per visit and the patient collection rate.
- Money is short at the end of a busy month. Track accounts receivable in days and the claim denial rate.
- Staff keep leaving. Track staff turnover and staff satisfaction.

Give each KPI an owner: a named person who works it out every month and is expected to say why it moved. A number nobody owns soon stops being worked out.

The 18 KPIs below are in six groups. Few clinics need all of them.

## The front desk and the day

| KPI | How to work it out | What it tells you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Average wait time | Minutes from check-in to the start of the consult, averaged over a week | Whether the day backs up at the desk or in one doctor's list |
| No-show rate | Booked patients who neither came nor cancelled ÷ all booked appointments × 100 | How many slots were lost that nobody else could use |
| Patients per clinical staff member | Patients seen in a day ÷ doctors and nurses on duty that day | Whether the team is stretched or waiting |

Say 20 patients were booked for a Monday and 3 neither came nor cancelled. The no-show rate for the day is 3 ÷ 20 × 100 = 15%. If the consultation fee is 500 rupees, those three slots are 1,500 rupees of fees the clinic did not earn that day.

Try appointment reminders first. EasyClinic sends [SMS and email appointment reminders](/features/appointment-scheduling/) on every plan, and WhatsApp reminders on Premium and Enterprise. We have no measured figure for their effect on no-shows, so we do not quote one. Work out your own no-show rate for a few months before and after you switch them on.

The wait time needs two times for each patient: when they checked in and when the consult started. If nobody records them today, ask the desk to write both in the register for one week each month.

## Money

| KPI | How to work it out | What it tells you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Revenue per visit | Total revenue in the period ÷ visits in the period | Whether a busier month also earned more |
| Cost per procedure | Consumables used + staff time for the procedure + a share of rent and running costs | Whether the price of a procedure covers what it costs |
| Operating margin | (Revenue − operating expenses) ÷ revenue × 100 | How much of every 100 earned is left after running costs |
| Accounts receivable, in days | Money owed to the clinic at month end ÷ average billed per day | How long the clinic waits to be paid |

Split revenue per visit by service if you can. The average hides which services carry the clinic and which ones lose money.

Staff time is the easiest part of cost per procedure to leave out. A 30-minute dressing done by a salaried nurse still uses half an hour of that salary.

For accounts receivable, say insurers and corporate clients owe the clinic 300,000 at the end of the month and the clinic bills 20,000 a day on average. The clinic is waiting 15 days for its money. If that figure grows month after month, look at who owes it before the cash runs short.

## Billing and claims

| KPI | How to work it out | What it tells you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Claim denial rate | Claims rejected ÷ claims submitted × 100 | How often a claim leaves the clinic with something wrong or missing |
| Patient collection rate | Money collected from patients ÷ money billed to patients × 100 | How much of what patients owe actually arrives |
| Time to bill | Hours or days from the end of the visit to the bill being raised | Whether services are billed while the visit is fresh |

In a clinic that bills at the desk as the patient leaves, time to bill should be close to zero. Where bills are written up later, a service forgotten on the day can be missed for good. Finding denied claims and money that never reached a bill is covered on the [revenue management page](/features/revenue-management/).

## Patients

| KPI | How to work it out | What it tells you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Patient satisfaction score | Average of a short rating after the visit, such as 1 to 5, or the share of patients who give 4 or 5 | How patients rate the visit, if enough of them answer |
| Patient retention rate | Patients seen last year who came back this year ÷ patients seen last year × 100 | Whether patients return for their next need |
| Follow-up adherence | Follow-up visits kept ÷ follow-up visits advised × 100 | Whether "come back in two weeks" actually happens |

A satisfaction score built from a handful of answers swings a lot from month to month, so read the comments as well as the number. Retention depends on the kind of clinic. A family doctor should see the same households year after year, and a surgeon may see a patient once, so compare your clinic with itself.

## Clinical

| KPI | How to work it out | What it tells you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Return visits for the same complaint | Patients who return with the same complaint within a set period, such as 14 days, ÷ all patients seen with that complaint × 100 | Whether the first visit solved the problem |
| An outcome measure for your specialty | Chosen by the doctors, for example the share of diabetic patients whose latest HbA1c is below the target the doctor set | Whether patients are getting better |

The first one is the clinic's version of a hospital's readmission rate. In a clinic some returns are planned, such as a wound check, so count only the unplanned ones. The outcome measure has to be one the doctors agree with, or nobody will act on it.

## Staff

| KPI | How to work it out | What it tells you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Staff turnover | Staff who left during the year ÷ average number of staff in the year × 100 | How often the clinic is hiring and training a replacement |
| Staff satisfaction | An anonymous survey twice a year, with the same questions each time | Whether problems are building before people resign |
| Training completion | Staff who finished required training on time ÷ staff who had to do it × 100 | Whether the training the clinic requires actually happens |

In a small clinic one resignation moves the turnover figure a long way, so look at the names behind it as well as the percentage.

## Setting targets

Use your own past months as the baseline. A published average is useful only if you know which clinics it came from and when.

A target needs a number and a date, and one person who owns it. "Bring the no-show rate from 15% to 10% by the end of March, owned by the front-desk lead" can be checked. "Fewer no-shows" cannot.

Change one thing at a time where you can. If the reminders and the booking rules both change in the same month and the no-show rate falls, you will not know which one worked.

## Review the numbers once a month

Keep the monthly review short, and hold it on the same day each month.

- Put every KPI you track on one page, with this month's figure next to its target. Keep last month's beside it so the direction is plain.
- For each KPI that moved the wrong way, agree one action and one person.
- Start the next review with last month's actions: did they happen, and did the number move?
- Once a quarter, drop any KPI nobody has acted on, and add one the clinic now needs.
