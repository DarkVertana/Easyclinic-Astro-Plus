Every clinic gets a bad review sooner or later: a long wait, a bill nobody explained, a receptionist having a bad day, or an outcome the patient did not expect. The review itself is out of your hands. The reply is yours, and it is read by two audiences, the person who wrote the review and every patient who looks you up afterwards.

This post covers what a reply should do, the line a doctor cannot cross in public, sample replies, and what Google will and will not remove.

## What a good reply does

A good reply is short and calm, and it would read the same whether or not the reviewer was ever your patient. It thanks the reviewer for writing, apologises for the experience they describe, says what the clinic is doing about that kind of problem, and gives a phone number or email to continue in private. Google's own [tips for replying](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3474122) say much the same, including taking a complicated case to phone or email.

Before you write, wait until you are calm and find out what happened. Check the appointment book and the bill, and ask the desk what they remember. Then decide who writes. One named person, usually the owner or the clinic manager, should read new reviews on a set day each week, and the doctor concerned should read any reply to a complaint about treatment before it goes out.

## The line a doctor cannot cross

A reply should not confirm that the reviewer is a patient, and should say nothing about a diagnosis, a treatment, a test, a date or a bill. That holds even when the reviewer has written about their own illness, and even when their account is wrong.

In India, regulation 7.14 of the [Indian Medical Council (Professional Conduct, Etiquette and Ethics) Regulations, 2002](https://nmc.org.in/page/rules-regulations-rules-regulations-of-erstwhile-mci-code-of-medical-ethics-regulations-2002), which the National Medical Commission [adopted in August 2023](https://www.nmc.org.in/storage/cms/rules-regulation-nmc/KEpwkMzgUwYNR49OrJaqvLcIpeytGPg7XGcorhTg.pdf), says a registered medical practitioner shall not disclose the secrets of a patient learnt in the exercise of the profession. The exceptions are a court order from the presiding judge and two public-safety cases: a serious and identified risk to a specific person or community, and notifiable diseases. Regulation 2.2 says confidences a patient entrusts to a physician should never be revealed unless the law requires it. A review reply is none of those exceptions. Check with your State Medical Council for any later change, and if you practise outside India, read what your own medical council's code says before you write the first reply.

Google's [reply tips](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3474122) also tell businesses never to share a reviewer's private information. They suggest addressing the reviewer by name, but in a clinic that can read as confirming the person was a patient, so leave it out.

## Sample replies

Write these before you need them, in your own words, and keep them to a few sentences. Replace the contact line with a real number or email.

**A long wait.** "Thank you for taking the time to write this. Waiting a long time without being told why is frustrating, and we are sorry. We are changing how the desk lets patients know about delays. If you would like to talk it through, please call the clinic manager on the clinic's number."

**A bill that was not explained.** "Thank you for your feedback. Charges should be explained before anyone pays, and we are sorry if that did not happen. Please call or email the clinic manager and we will go through it with you."

**Rudeness at the desk.** "Thank you for telling us. Everyone who contacts the clinic should be treated with courtesy, and we have shared this with the team. We would like to hear more, so please call the clinic manager directly."

**A complaint about treatment.** "We are sorry to read this. We cannot discuss anyone's care in public, and that applies to every review on this page. Please call the clinic and ask for the doctor, who will speak with you."

**A review you think is fake.** Reply with the same neutral wording, so readers see the clinic answer, and report it to Google if it breaks Google's policies (see below). Do not say in public that the person was never a patient.

## Take it offline, then close the loop

When the reviewer calls, listen before you explain, and let them finish. If the clinic got something wrong, say so. If it did not, explain what you can do and what you cannot, which is also Google's advice.

Google notifies the reviewer when you reply, and the reviewer can still change the review afterwards, according to its [Business Profile help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3474050). That is their choice to make. Google's [policy](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114) forbids offering anything, such as a discount or a free visit, in exchange for revising or removing a negative review, and reviews posted after such an offer are removed. You can edit or delete your own reply later if the matter is resolved.

## When to report a review to Google

A business cannot delete a review. It can report one, and Google removes it only if it breaks Google's content policies. Google's [policy page](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114) lists what is not allowed, including content not based on a real experience, reviews posted from several accounts by one person, reviews from someone with a conflict of interest such as a competitor or a former employee, harassment and hate speech.

Google's [help page on reporting](https://support.google.com/business/answer/4596773) says not to report a review just because you disagree with it, and that Google does not get involved in disputes between businesses and customers. You report a review from your Business Profile and follow its status in the Reviews Management Tool. If Google finds no violation, you can appeal once. If someone threatens a clinic with negative reviews unless it pays, the same page points to a separate way to report the extortion attempt.

## Asking for reviews without breaking Google's rules

Google's [review tips](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3474122) say a mix of positive and negative feedback often feels more trustworthy to readers, but how a clinic asks for reviews matters. Google's [policy](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114) forbids selectively asking happy patients for reviews, offering incentives for reviews, pressuring customers to write one while they are on the premises and asking staff to collect a set number of reviews.

So ask every patient the same way, for example with a review link or QR code at the desk, which the same tips suggest, and let them write it at home if they want to. How reviews shape which clinic a patient picks is covered in [Google reviews for clinics](/google-reviews-for-clinics/).

## Hear the complaint before it goes online

The usual complaints are about the wait, the bill, the tone at the desk, a gap in communication, or treatment that did not go as the patient expected. Each of these can be heard inside the clinic first.

- Ask after every visit. A short survey gives an unhappy patient somewhere to say so. EasyClinic's patient engagement tools include a feedback survey after the visit, whose ratings come back to the clinic to act on.
- Call patients after a procedure or a hard diagnosis, the next day, to ask how they are and whether anything in the plan is unclear.
- Tell patients about delays as they happen, and explain charges before the patient pays.
- Keep a log of complaints, from reviews and from the desk, by type. Once a month, look at the biggest group and change one thing.

Handling the unhappy patient in person matters as much as the online reply. Train the desk to listen without arguing, and to fetch the manager or doctor when the patient wants more than the desk can give. Our post on [patient communication techniques](/effective-patient-communication-techniques/) covers the words that help.

## What not to do

| Mistake | Why it hurts |
| --- | --- |
| Replying in anger | The reviewer and every later reader see it, long after the clinic has calmed down |
| Ignoring the review | Readers see a complaint with no answer from the clinic |
| Mentioning the visit or the diagnosis | It breaks the doctor's duty of confidentiality |
| Blaming the patient | Other patients read it as how the clinic will treat them |
| Offering something to change the review | Google forbids it and removes reviews posted after such an offer |
| Reporting every bad review | Google removes only reviews that break its policies |
