A video call with a patient is a consultation under Kenyan law, and the Digital Health Act puts duties on the provider for each one. This post sets out what the law asks of a clinic that adds teleconsultations, from who may offer them to what happens to the record afterwards. Each point links to its source. Read those, and speak to the KMPDC or a lawyer before you rely on this summary.

## What the law says about telemedicine

The [Health Act 2017](https://machakosassembly.go.ke/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Health-Act-21-of-2017.pdf) made e-health "a recognized mode of health service" in section 103. Section 104 asked the Cabinet Secretary to bring in a law on e-health, with telemedicine among the things it had to cover.

The detailed rules are now in the [Digital Health Act 2023](https://nhts.dha.go.ke/api/file-download/?filename=Digital+Health+Act+15+of+2023.pdf), which came into force on 2 November 2023. Part VI of the Act covers e-health service delivery:

- Section 40 makes e-health a recognised model of health service delivery, and says e-health services complement the existing ways of delivering care.
- Section 41 lists how e-health is provided: telemedicine, electronic health records, m-health, e-learning, telehealth and other recognised e-health services.
- The Act defines telemedicine as providing health care and sharing medical knowledge over distance by telecommunications. Telehealth is the wider term, and includes video conferencing and store-and-forward imaging.

Older articles, including an earlier version of this one, describe Kenya's telemedicine rules as still missing, and the KMPDC said [in 2021](https://kmpdc.go.ke/2021/02/28/telemedicine-gains-pace-in-kenya-as-kmpdc-approves-20-hospitals-to-roll-out-services-amid-covid-19-containment-measures/) that Kenya was yet to enact laws regulating telemedicine. That changed with the Digital Health Act.

## Who may offer teleconsultations

Section 41(2) of the [Digital Health Act](https://nhts.dha.go.ke/api/file-download/?filename=Digital+Health+Act+15+of+2023.pdf) says an entity providing e-health services must be one of these:

- a healthcare provider with a valid licence from the relevant regulatory body;
- a healthcare provider licensed by an equivalent authority outside Kenya, and recognised by the local regulator;
- a health facility licensed by the relevant regulatory body to offer e-health services;
- a facility outside Kenya, licensed by an equivalent authority that Kenya recognises.

A clinic whose doctors hold current KMPDC practice licences starts from the first line. The KMPDC also publishes an [application for registration as a virtual medical services provider](https://kmpdc.go.ke/resources/Application%20for%20Registration%20as%20a%20Virtual%20Medical%20Services%20Provider.pdf). A medical director registrable with a Kenyan regulatory body fills it in. The form names the licensed "link" facility behind the virtual service, and asks for the current practice licence of every doctor who will consult through it.

The medical director signs a declaration that:

- every practitioner meets the registration and licensing requirements and works within their scope of practice;
- the medical director keeps a list of the licensed practitioners who provide virtual services;
- the virtual facility keeps a record of all services provided;
- personal health information is not used for research without ethical approval;
- the Data Protection Act and the Ministry of Health's policies on telemedicine and e-health are followed.

In its [2021 notice](https://kmpdc.go.ke/2021/02/28/telemedicine-gains-pace-in-kenya-as-kmpdc-approves-20-hospitals-to-roll-out-services-amid-covid-19-containment-measures/), the council said it was issuing annual licences to facilities to offer virtual consultations, once it was satisfied the service was aligned to the data protection and medical records rules. The form itself does not say which clinics must file it. Ask the KMPDC before your first teleconsultation, and keep the answer on file.

## What the Act asks of every teleconsultation

Section 43(1) of the [Digital Health Act](https://nhts.dha.go.ke/api/file-download/?filename=Digital+Health+Act+15+of+2023.pdf) lists what a healthcare provider must do when providing e-health services to a patient:

- give the patient the information they need to manage their health;
- make sure the patient can reach their own health records where necessary;
- manage the patient's data as the law prescribes;
- deliver the highest possible quality of care;
- make sure the provider's agents follow the Act;
- use a platform that is interoperable with the integrated health information system that section 15 of the Act sets up;
- get the consent of a parent or appointed guardian when the patient is a minor;
- get the consent of an appointed guardian or next friend when the patient is mentally ill.

Section 43(2) adds that sharing a patient's images and lab results for consultation or training must follow the standards the law prescribes. Section 41(3) asks the Cabinet Secretary to develop standards and guidelines for e-health platforms, so ask your software vendor how its platform meets the interoperability duty.

Two of these need planning before the first call. For a child, someone at the clinic has to know who the adult on the call is and record their consent. For a follow-up, the patient should be able to get their record, so decide how they will ask for it.

## Patient data under the Data Protection Act

The [Data Protection Act 2019](https://www.odpc.go.ke/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/TheDataProtectionAct__No24of2019.pdf) treats a person's health status as sensitive personal data, and its definition of health data includes data collected while registering a patient or providing health services. For a clinic, that covers the booking, the call and the notes.

What it asks of a clinic:

- **Registration.** Section 18 says no one may act as a data controller unless registered with the Data Commissioner. The [2021 registration regulations](https://www.odpc.go.ke/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/THE-DATA-PROTECTION-REGISTRATION-OF-DATA-CONTROLLERS-AND-DATA-PROCESSORS-REGULATIONS-2021.pdf) exempt small businesses, but not those processing data for health administration and patient care. The ODPC's [guidance note on health data](https://www.odpc.go.ke/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/ODPC-Guidance-Note-on-Processing-of-Health-Data.pdf) says every entity in the healthcare sector must register.
- **Who handles health data.** Under section 46, health data may be processed only by or under the responsibility of a health care provider, or by someone with a legal duty of professional secrecy.
- **The patient's rights.** Section 26 gives patients the right to be told how their data will be used, to see it, to object to its use, and to have false or misleading data corrected or deleted.
- **Breaches.** Under section 43, when data is accessed by someone unauthorised and there is a real risk of harm, the clinic notifies the Data Commissioner within 72 hours of becoming aware of it and tells the patient in writing.

The [Digital Health Act](https://nhts.dha.go.ke/api/file-download/?filename=Digital+Health+Act+15+of+2023.pdf) adds its own duties, and section 61 says anyone processing personal data under it must comply with the Data Protection Act. A patient may ask in writing for a copy of their personal health information (section 36). Before releasing health data, the clinic must be satisfied about the identity of the person asking, and take reasonable steps so that only that person, or someone with authority to act for them, receives it (section 38). Section 35 makes it an offence to lose health data or to share sensitive personal data with an unauthorised party. The penalty can reach a fine of one million shillings and fifteen years in prison, but where the offence involves sensitive personal data, the penalties of the Data Protection Act apply instead.

In practice, if the prescription or a report goes out on WhatsApp or by email after the call, check that the number or the address belongs to the patient before you press send.

## Keep the teleconsultation on the patient's record

The KMPDC declaration asks the virtual service to keep a record of every service it provides, and the Digital Health Act expects the patient to be able to reach their record. The simplest way to meet both is to treat the video call like a visit. Book it where the clinic's visits are booked, and write the note and the prescription on the same patient record, marked as a remote consultation.

A record kept on the doctor's own phone, in a chat thread, does neither. Nobody at the clinic can find it at the next visit, and it leaves the clinic's data on a personal device.

Keep in mind what the Act says about e-health complementing clinic care. Some complaints need hands and a stethoscope. When the doctor decides a patient needs to be seen, book the clinic visit before the call ends.
