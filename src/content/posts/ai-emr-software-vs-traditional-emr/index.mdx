In this post, a traditional EMR is one without AI: the doctor types the note or builds it from templates and shortcuts. An AI EMR adds a scribe that listens to the consult and drafts the note for the doctor to check. Both keep the record on a screen instead of on a shelf, so the time question is narrower than it first looks. It comes down to who writes the note, and how long the doctor then spends checking what the software wrote.

## Where an AI scribe saves time

The research below is about the note.

A [commentary in npj Digital Medicine](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-025-01895-6) summed up earlier studies as suggesting that AI scribes can cut documentation time by 20% to 30%. In the same paragraph it warned that the speed of adoption has outpaced validation and oversight.

A 2025 study published in [JAMA Network Open](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12492056/) asked doctors and other clinicians in US health systems how much time they spent each week writing notes outside clinic hours, before and after they started using the same ambient AI scribe. Their answers went down, and so did the share who reported burnout.

Read that result for what it is. The answers are the clinicians' own estimates on a survey, not timed minutes, and there was no group without the scribe to compare with. Many of the clinicians enrolled did not complete both surveys. The scribe company helped collect the data, although the paper says it had no role in the analysis.

For your clinic, the saving depends on where your notes get written today. A doctor who finishes short notes during the consult, with shortcuts for the usual prescriptions, has less to save than one who types long notes at 9pm. If your evenings go to notes, an AI scribe is aimed at your problem. If they do not, most of the time an EMR saves you comes from the EMR itself.

EasyClinic has not measured how many minutes Cura AI saves per note, so we do not quote a figure.

## Where the time goes, and which kind of EMR saves it

| Job in the day | An EMR without AI | What an AI scribe changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Finding the patient's last visit | The record opens by name, with past visits and medicines | Nothing |
| Writing the note | Typed, or built from templates and shortcuts | Drafted from the conversation, then read and corrected by the doctor |
| The prescription | Built from shortcuts for the medicines you usually give | Some scribes, Cura AI among them, draft it with the note for the doctor to check |
| Reminding patients to come back | A reminder sent from the booked appointment | Nothing |
| The owner's end-of-day numbers | Reports built from what was recorded during the day | Nothing |

Much of the time an EMR saves over paper sits in the rows where the AI changes nothing. If your clinic is still on paper, that move is the bigger change, and our guide to [EMR vs paper records](/easyclinic-emr-vs-traditional-emr/) covers it.

## What the doctor still has to check

A drafted note is not a finished note. The npj Digital Medicine commentary lists the ways AI scribes go wrong. They can add findings that never happened, such as an examination that was not done, leave out something the patient said, misread the context of a remark, and attribute the patient's words to the doctor or the other way round. It also reports that speech recognition makes more errors with some speakers and accents.

Two practical points follow. The doctor has to read every draft before confirming it, and that reading time comes off the saving. A scribe tested on American English consults may also do worse with a consult that moves between English and Hindi, or English and Swahili, so try any scribe on your own patients before you rely on it.

In Cura AI, a drafted note stays a suggestion until the doctor confirms it, and the doctor can change anything first. The note that goes into the record is still the doctor's note.

## When an EMR without AI is enough

Some clinics will get little from an AI scribe:

- Doctors whose notes are short and written during the consult. Shortcuts already do most of the work. EasyClinic's promise to doctors moving off pen and paper is that two hours, or ten patient records, makes prescription writing faster than it was on paper, and that promise is about the EMR itself.
- Clinics whose internet drops often. Cura AI needs a connection, and EasyClinic does not run fully offline today.
- Consults in a language or accent the scribe handles badly. Test it on your own patients before you plan a busy clinic around it.
- Doctors who would rather write than read. Checking a draft is a different habit from writing a note, and it suits some doctors better than others.

In EasyClinic the AI is optional. The EMR works without it, and you write the note and the prescription yourself in the same chart.

## How to measure it in your own clinic

Nobody can give you a per-note figure that fits your clinic, so measure your own. It takes two weeks.

1. For one normal week, write down when each doctor finishes the last note of the day, and how many patients they saw.
2. Switch the scribe on the next week, with the same doctors and a similar list of patients.
3. Write down the same two numbers, and count the drafts the doctor had to correct in a way that mattered.
4. Compare the finishing times, and ask each doctor whether the corrections were worth it.

If the last note of the day moves from 9pm to the end of clinic hours, the scribe is giving the doctor evenings back. If the finishing time does not move, the checking is eating the saving.
