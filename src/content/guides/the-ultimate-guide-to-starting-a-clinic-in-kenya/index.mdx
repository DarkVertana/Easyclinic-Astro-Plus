This guide follows the order you will meet things in: the area, the premises, the fit-out, the people and then the paperwork. The licensing steps are gathered into one numbered checklist further down, next to the fees this guide quotes in Kenyan shillings. Check each fee against the regulator's current schedule before you budget, because fees change and a guide cannot promise they have not.

## Why open a clinic in Kenya

Kenya has too few doctors for its patients and too few facilities near where people live. Public facilities are overstretched while the towns keep growing, so a well-run private clinic in the right neighbourhood does not usually want for patients. County governments also work with private clinics on referrals and outreach, which gives a new clinic a way in with the local health team.

For many doctors and nurses, a clinic comes after years of training and employment, and it is tempting to wait until there is more money in the bank. Many clinics in Kenya started small and added services as patients came back. If you already meet the licensing and experience requirements below, waiting usually costs more than it saves.

## Start with a market survey

Before you sign anything, find out four things about the area you want to serve:

- What it will cost: registration and licensing, rent, equipment and staff salaries.
- Which rules apply: the national licensing steps below, plus your county's own rules.
- Who you need to hire, and which registration each of them must hold.
- What patients in the area are missing, and who already serves them.

Walk the street in the morning and again in the evening. If the clinics are all bunched on one road and the next estate has none, that tells you more than a report will.

## Decide what kind of clinic you are opening

The type of clinic decides who you hire and what you buy, so settle it first.

- A general clinic offers basic care for common illnesses.
- A specialised clinic focuses on one area, such as [paediatrics](/pediatric-emr/), dentistry or orthopaedics.
- A consultation-based clinic brings in specialist doctors for more advanced care.

Open in the field you trained in. If you are a dentist, open a dental clinic. Your qualifications are what make the care safe, and the council will ask for certified copies of them when you apply for the licence.

## Who can own and run a clinic

The person in charge of the clinic must be a trained medical professional with a valid licence. Anyone, medical or not, can register a clinic with the Kenya Medical Practitioners and Dentists Council (KMPDC), but only qualified medical staff approved by their own regulator can manage the patients. For a Level 3B facility the person in charge must be a medical officer with at least three years of practical experience, [by the KMPDC's own notice](https://kmpdc.go.ke/2025/02/12/who-can-own-or-register-a-health-institution/).

If you are not a medical professional, you can still own the clinic. Hire a qualified, registered doctor to manage the clinical side and run the business yourself.

Either way, a clinic is a business. Someone has to keep the accounts, pay the staff, order stock and bring patients in. If that someone is you and you have never done it, consider a short business course. If there is no time for one, a business consultant can cover the gap while you learn.

## The regulators you will deal with

The [KMPDC](https://kmpdc.go.ke/), called the Medical Practitioners and Dentists Board in older guides, registers doctors and dentists and licenses the clinic itself, and it runs an online portal for licensing. The other bodies depend on who works in your clinic and what services you offer:

- Nursing Council of Kenya (NCK): registers nurses.
- Clinical Officers Council: registers clinical officers.
- Pharmacy and Poisons Board: regulates drugs.
- Kenya Medical Laboratory Technicians and Technologists Board: oversees laboratories, if you run one.
- Physiotherapy Council of Kenya: for physiotherapy services.
- Nutritionists and Dieticians Institute: for nutrition services.

Your county government sets its own rules as well, including licensing and waste management. Ask at the county office what applies to your area before you sign a lease.

## Write a business plan

A business plan makes you answer the questions a bank or a partner will ask anyway. Keep it to five parts:

- Mission and vision: why this clinic, in this place.
- Services: what you will offer on the first day and what comes later.
- Target market: who will walk through the door, and how they will pay.
- Financial plan: what setting up costs and where the money comes from, set against your revenue goals.
- Marketing: how the neighbourhood finds out you are open.

If you have never written one, start from an online template, or pay a business plan writer and spend the time you save on the premises.

## Choose the premises

The location decides your patient numbers more than anything else you control. Look for a busy area with plenty of households around it. Before you sign, check four things:

- Drainage and easy access, with parking and room to grow.
- Running water and electricity, a toilet, and enough light and ventilation for the inspection.
- An incineration facility nearby for clinical waste. If there is none, sign an agreement with the county hospital.
- The competition: who is already there, and what you will do differently.

Rent is the cost you are stuck with longest, so choose an affordable location and be ready to trade size for position. Then sign a lease of 5 to 10 years, so you are not moving just as patients learn where you are.

## Fit out the clinic the way the inspection expects

Two services are mandatory: basic outpatient care, meaning general check-ups and treatment, and emergency care, which means an emergency tray and a first aid kit on hand. Minor procedures such as suturing belong on the list too. Basic laboratory tests, like malaria or HIV tests, and community outreach are optional.

Plan the rooms around patient flow and privacy. The layout needs:

- A reception area where patients are received.
- A pay point, or teller.
- A consulting room.
- A treatment, observation and procedure area.
- A health information system for keeping records, on paper or on a computer.
- Storage cabinets for drugs and supplies.
- At least three waste segregation bins: infectious, non-infectious and sharps.
- Proof of a contract with a licensed waste disposal company.
- Good lighting and ventilation.
- CCTV, which is optional.

If the space allows, partition off an administrative office, a laboratory (only if you offer tests), a ward for observation and a storage room.

## Equipment to buy

Clinics in Kenya mostly handle outpatient cases, so buy for that. Use the KMPDC inspection checklist as your shopping list and leave hospital-grade machines, such as an ECG, for later. Buy affordable equipment that lasts and meets KMPDC and Pharmacy and Poisons Board standards. For drugs and consumables, set up 3 or 4 reliable suppliers.

### Examination and diagnosis

- Stethoscope
- Blood pressure monitor, manual or digital
- Digital or infrared thermometer
- Pulse oximeter
- Glucometer, for diabetic patients
- Otoscope, useful in a paediatric clinic
- Ophthalmoscope
- Weighing scale and height measure
- Tongue depressors and a penlight

### Minor procedures

- Sutures, needles and suture removal kits
- Forceps, scissors and needle holders
- Local anaesthetic (lidocaine) and syringes
- Gauze, bandages and adhesive tape
- Antiseptics such as iodine, saline and wound dressings

### Emergency and resuscitation

- Emergency drugs, including adrenaline and antihistamines
- A small oxygen cylinder, masks and nasal cannulas
- A bag-valve-mask resuscitator
- A stocked first aid kit
- An emergency tray with syringes, needles and IV cannulas

### Medicines

- Essential drugs: painkillers such as paracetamol, antibiotics such as amoxicillin, antimalarials and blood pressure drugs
- Vaccines, for example tetanus and flu
- IV fluids (saline), in limited quantities
- Syringes and needles
- Over-the-counter medicines for colds, allergies and pain

### Furniture and consumables

- An examination table
- Chairs for the waiting area and for staff
- Cabinets for supplies and records
- Disposable gloves, masks and gowns
- Hand sanitiser, disinfectant and soap
- Sharps containers alongside the three waste bins

### Extras by clinic type

- Paediatric clinic: a paediatric stethoscope, growth charts and child-friendly thermometers.
- Dental clinic: a dental chair, mirrors and probes, and sterilisation equipment.
- Basic laboratory: a microscope, malaria and HIV test kits, and a small centrifuge.

### Other things to buy

- Fire extinguishers and exit signs
- Signs that direct patients to reception
- Spare gloves, masks and aprons
- Desks and shelves for the admin office
- A records system, either paper files or [clinic software](/features/emr/)
- Brochures for the launch

## Hire registered staff

Every clinician you hire must be trained and registered. Doctors register with the KMPDC and nurses with the NCK, while clinical officers have their own council. Look for at least two years of experience in the health field. The core team is small:

- A doctor registered with the KMPDC.
- A nurse registered with the NCK.
- An office administrator.
- Lab technicians or clinical officers, if your services need them.

Ask a doctor or nurse you trust to sit in on the interviews. They will hear the gaps in a clinical answer that you might miss. Collect certified copies of each person's qualifications when you hire them, because they go into your licensing pack.

## Register, inspect and license the clinic

Most of the wait at this stage is for other people's calendars, so do it in this order.

### Register the business

Register the business first and keep the business registration certificate. The council asks for it along with everything else.

### Pass the sub-county inspection

The sub-county (district) health management team, the DHMT, inspects every new clinic.

1. Go to the sub-county office and introduce yourself to the DHMT.
2. Book a pre-inspection with the public health officer or the public health nurse. Treat it as a rehearsal and fix what they point out.
3. Pay the inspection fee of KES 1,000 to 2,000 and book the official inspection with the DHMT.
4. Fix everything in the inspection report, and keep copies of the signed report.

### Submit the documents and get the licence

With the signed report in hand, put the pack together for the KMPDC, or for the Clinical Officers Council if a clinical officer runs the clinic (the NCK if your licence comes through the nursing council):

- Form IX, filled in and signed by a medical officer, with any other forms the area medical officer must sign.
- The inspection report, signed by the inspection team.
- Your business registration certificate.
- Certified copies of staff qualifications, such as the Private Practice Licence.
- Building plans, only if the clinic has a mortuary.
- The fees in the table below.

The licence usually arrives three to four weeks after you submit, and you can download it online. From then on, you renew it every year.

## What the paperwork costs

These are the fees this guide quotes, stage by stage.

| Stage | Fee | When you pay |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Application to register the clinic | KES 1,000 | With the application to the council |
| Sub-county inspection | KES 1,000 to 2,000 | When you book the inspection |
| Annual facility licence (medical or dental clinic) | KES 15,000 | Every year |

The KMPDC also charges a registration fee set by the facility's level and type. Its [registration page](https://kmpdc.go.ke/registration-of-a-health-facility/) does not give the amount, so ask the council for it before you budget. The KES 15,000 licence fee comes from the council's [2025 licence fee list](https://kmpdc.go.ke/licensing-of-a-health-facility/); fees change, so check it again before you commit.

The paperwork is a small part of the bill. Setting up the whole clinic typically costs KES 300,000 to KES 1.5 million, depending on the location and size of the premises and the equipment you buy. ## Licensing checklist

1. Register the business and keep the registration certificate.
2. Check your county's own licensing and waste rules for your area.
3. Make sure every clinician is registered. Doctors and dentists register with the [KMPDC](https://kmpdc.go.ke/) and nurses with the Nursing Council of Kenya; clinical officers register with the Clinical Officers Council.
4. Sign a contract with a licensed waste disposal company, and an agreement with the county hospital if there is no incineration facility nearby.
5. Visit the sub-county office and meet the DHMT.
6. Book the pre-inspection with the public health officer or public health nurse, and fix what they find.
7. Pay the KES 1,000 to 2,000 inspection fee and book the official DHMT inspection.
8. Fix everything in the inspection report and keep copies of the signed report.
9. Get Form IX filled in and signed by a medical officer.
10. Collect certified copies of staff qualifications, and building plans if the clinic has a mortuary.
11. Submit the pack and the fees to the [KMPDC](https://kmpdc.go.ke/), or to the [Clinical Officers Council](https://clinicalofficerscouncil.org/) if a clinical officer runs the clinic. A clinic licensed through the nursing council submits to the NCK instead.
12. Download the licence online when it is issued, usually three to four weeks later.
13. Once you are trading, file your returns with the [Kenya Revenue Authority](https://www.kra.go.ke/) on time.
14. Renew the licence every year; the KMPDC's 2025 fee for a medical or dental clinic is KES 15,000.

## Running the clinic once you are licensed

The licence is the start. What keeps a clinic open is ordinary discipline:

- Follow clinical guidelines and evidence-based care, and give each patient real time.
- Keep drug stocks with 3 or 4 reliable suppliers, so one late delivery does not empty a shelf.
- Track equipment and inventory, in software or in a stock book.
- Record income and expenses every day, and file KRA returns on time.
- Pay staff on time, every month.
- Call patients after treatment to ask how they are doing.

Build a few relationships early: the county hospital, which can take your clinical waste, and the DHMT, your first stop for compliance questions. Good suppliers of drugs and equipment matter as much. If you are not a doctor, a clinical partner can bring the medical expertise, and some partners bring funding as well.

To find patients, print flyers and brochures for the surrounding estates and approach local companies about treating their staff under their insurance plans. Make the waiting area somewhere people do not mind sitting. After that, patient education and follow-up calls keep people coming back. In Kenya, word of mouth is still one of the strongest ways a clinic grows.

## Where new clinics go wrong

The same four mistakes come up again and again.

1. Treating clinical skill as enough. Compliance and money need real time every week, on top of the patients.
2. Overspending on equipment. Most clinics in Kenya handle outpatient cases, and an expensive machine that sits idle is a debt with no income behind it.
3. Choosing the wrong spot. An area crowded with competitors and an area with too little footfall both slow you down. Close to homes and transport, in an underserved area, tends to work better.
4. Ignoring the waiting room. Long waits and no follow-up call lose patients even when the medicine is good.

The clinics that break even fastest tend to focus on outpatient care, chronic disease management, maternal care and prevention, where demand is steady and running costs are lower than for inpatient or specialist services. Managed well, a clinic can be profitable within 12 to 24 months. Clinics that get through the first two years often become a stable income for their owners.

## Choosing software before opening day

Decide how you will keep records before the inspection, because the layout needs a health information system and the pay point needs a way to take money. Paper works, until the receipt book and the stock card stop agreeing. Clinics that keep the records and the till in one system lose less revenue, and the doctor sees more patients in a day. If you are going to use software, choose it before the first patient. Choose it later and the paper records have to be moved across.
