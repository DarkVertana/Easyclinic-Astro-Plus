This guide follows the order the work comes in, starting with the area and the type of clinic and ending with the paperwork. The licensing steps are collected into one numbered checklist near the end, next to the costs in Ugandan shillings. Fees change, so check each one with the office that charges it before you budget.

## Why open a clinic in Uganda

Uganda, with a population of over 50 million, has more patients than its public facilities can see. [WHO figures](https://www.who.int/data/gho/data/indicators/indicator-details/GHO/medical-doctors-%28per-10-000-population%29) put the country at about 0.2 doctors for every 1,000 people in 2024, roughly one doctor for every 5,000 people. The 2023/24 national budget gave health 6% of the total, according to Trade.gov. Government hospitals are crowded, the waits are long, the pharmacies run out of drugs and there are too few staff. Patients who can pay a fair fee go to private clinics for shorter waits and care they can predict.

Kampala needs more clinics. So do fast-growing towns such as Mbarara and Gulu, and the rural districts that still have few doctors. Community health insurance and NGO-supported programmes also bring in patients on low incomes, and clinics that accept insurance and post their prices see patients all year.

You do not need a hospital to meet that demand. Many doctors open a small clinic offering outpatient care, maternal services, basic diagnostics and a pharmacy, and expand into fuller diagnostics or teleconsultation once patients keep coming back. They make money on steady patient numbers rather than on expensive procedures. A well-run small clinic often breaks even within 12 to 18 months, although nothing guarantees it.

## Start with a market survey

Before you sign a lease, learn how care is organised where you plan to open. Uganda's public system runs from Village Health Teams (VHTs), who give basic care in the community, up to Health Centre IVs, which offer more advanced services, with the district level and the national referral hospitals above them. Private clinics work in towns and villages alike, and your competition is whichever of these patients use now.

Find out four things about the area:

- What patients go without. In rural areas it is often maternal care, because midwives are overstretched. In Kampala, non-communicable diseases are rising.
- Where they go instead. Many people self-medicate or see traditional healers.
- Who already serves them, and what those clinics do not offer.
- What it will cost you. The tables further down list fees, rent, equipment and salaries.

Run a small survey of your own, and visit each possible site more than once. A street that is busy on market day can be quiet for the rest of the week.

## Decide what kind of clinic you are opening

The type of clinic decides who you hire and what you buy, so settle it before anything else.

- General practice, for the widest range of patients.
- Paediatrics, for families with children.
- Diagnostics, where demand in the towns is growing.
- A mobile clinic, to reach remote areas with portable equipment.

Match the clinic to your training. If the area needs maternal care and you are not a midwife, bring in one, or partner with a specialist. Antenatal care is the gap in the rural north, where midwives are overstretched. In Kampala, diagnostics are in demand as urban diseases rise.

Start small. A small clinic can open on UGX 2 million to 5 million, which lets you test the market before you commit more. Public-private partnerships (PPPs) can help later, with funding or infrastructure when you want to expand.

## Qualifications, and who can own a clinic

Every clinician needs a qualification and a council registration. The foundation is an MBBS, or a nursing or midwifery diploma, plus the internship. Doctors then register with the Uganda Medical and Dental Practitioners Council (UMDPC), and nurses and midwives with the Uganda Nurses and Midwives Council (UNMC). It takes years, and there is no shortcut.

A doctor's annual practising licence costs UGX 100,000 to 200,000. Let it lapse and you cannot practise legally, so renew it on time.

You do not have to be a doctor to own a clinic in Uganda. All medical services must be supervised by practitioners licensed by the UMDPC or UNMC, so an owner without a clinical background hires one to run the clinical side and runs the business. The UMDPC asks for a supervising doctor who has been licensed without interruption for at least three consecutive years.

Allied health staff must be registered with their own council as well. Extra skills pay off: a clinician who can do ultrasound is valuable in rural areas where diagnostics are scarce. Keep training everyone, because health workers are in short supply and a trained one is hard to replace.

## The regulators you will deal with

- Uganda Registration Services Bureau (URSB): registers the business.
- UMDPC: registers and licenses doctors, and registers and licenses private health units after an inspection checklist signed by the District Health Office or a UMDPC inspector ([requirements](https://umdpc.go.ug/downloads/requirements/requirements.pdf)).
- UNMC: registers and licenses nurses and midwives.
- National Drug Authority (NDA): regulates drugs and approves the clinic's drug storage.
- Ministry of Health: sets the facility standards the inspectors check against.
- Kampala Capital City Authority (KCCA) in Kampala, or the municipal council elsewhere: issues the local permit.

The clinic must also follow the rules on medical waste disposal and on patient data privacy, and the waste needs environmental clearance. The rules are enforced. Clinics that ignored them have been fined or closed after audits, so ask each office what applies to you before you sign a lease.

## Write a business plan

A business plan makes you answer the questions a bank or a partner will ask anyway. Keep it to four parts:

- Target market: the urban middle class who want better care, or an underserved rural area. Pick one and shape the services around it.
- Money: a start-up budget of UGX 2 million to 20 million, depending on size and services, set against the patients you expect each month.
- Marketing: signage and a website, costed in the table below.
- Premises: the rooms you need on the first day, and room to grow.

Aim to break even within 12 to 18 months. Once you open, compare the forecast with the real cash flow every month and adjust.

## Choose the premises

Location decides your patient numbers more than anything else you control. Check these before you sign:

- Access. Good roads, so patients can reach you. If the area is too remote for a fixed clinic, a mobile clinic may serve it better.
- Rent. Expect UGX 200,000 to 800,000 in Kampala, or UGX 80,000 to 200,000 in a rural district, where communities are more underserved.
- Visibility. A busy spot with room for a sign matters most where other clinics are nearby.
- Power and water. Plan for a generator or solar panels, and a reliable water supply. Outages are common in rural areas, and a clinic without light or water cannot see patients.
- Footfall. Visit each site several times, and watch how many patients go to the clinics already there.

## Services and layout

Start with the services in highest demand:

- General consultations.
- Antenatal care.
- Basic laboratory tests.
- Minor procedures.
- Essential pharmacy services.

Plan the rooms around patient flow and privacy. The layout needs a waiting area where patients can sit comfortably, two or three consultation rooms, a pharmacy and a place for emergency equipment, including a defibrillator. Decide early whether the records go on paper or into software. Solar power or a generator, and water on tap for hygiene, matter in the towns as much as in rural areas.

## Equipment and supplies

- Basic equipment, UGX 800,000 to 4 million: stethoscopes and blood pressure monitors for routine checks, and an autoclave for sterilising.
- Advanced equipment, UGX 2 million to 8 million: an ultrasound machine for prenatal care and an ECG for heart monitoring.
- First stock, UGX 400,000 to 1.2 million: medicines for common illnesses, and syringes.
- Emergency equipment: a defibrillator and basic emergency tools.

Leave the advanced machines until patient numbers justify them. Buy from local dealers when you need something quickly, or lease through a PPP. Much of the equipment is imported, so spare parts can be slow to arrive. Keep a maintenance schedule and train one member of staff to handle basic repairs.

## Hire registered staff

A new clinic can open with a small team and grow to four or five staff as patient numbers rise.

- A doctor to lead clinical care, registered with the UMDPC.
- A nurse, at UGX 200,000 to 400,000 a month, registered with the UNMC.
- A midwife for maternal services, at UGX 150,000 to 300,000 a month, also registered with the UNMC.
- Allied health staff, registered with their own council, if your services need them.

Check every licence before you make an offer. Train the team on your clinical protocols and on the records system, and offer incentives such as bonuses. Every nurse who leaves is a gap a small clinic has to fill in a hurry.

## Register, inspect and license the clinic

Most of the time at this stage goes on waiting for other offices, so start early and work in this order.

### Register the business

Register the business with the URSB, with the business details and ownership documents. Registration costs UGX 100,000 to 500,000 and takes one to two weeks if your ID and proof of address are ready. Keep the registration certificate and the URSB extract, because the councils ask for both.

Then get a tax identification number (TIN). It is free, and it is mandatory. If you are after government-backed funding through a PPP, a consultant who knows the process can save you time.

### Get the local permit

In Kampala the permit comes from KCCA; elsewhere it comes from the municipal council. It costs UGX 50,000 to 200,000 depending on the area, and it involves a local health check.

### Prepare for the inspection

Agree the inspection date with the UMDPC or UNMC and the local authority, and give yourself one to two weeks to get ready. The inspectors check the clinic against the Ministry of Health's standards. Keep the rooms clean and the fire exits clear. Switch on every piece of equipment yourself before they arrive.

Run a mock inspection of your own first. Walk through the clinic as an inspector would and look for what catches clinics out, such as a power cut with no backup or a lapse in sanitation. If the inspectors raise concerns, fix them within one to two weeks so the approval is not held up.

### Submit the documents

Put the pack together:

- The business registration certificate.
- The URSB extract.
- The annual practising licence for each clinician.
- Diploma transcripts for each qualification.

File the pack with the councils and with the NDA. Budget UGX 500,000 to 1.5 million in total for these filings. Follow up with each office until the approvals come through. After that, some licences renew every year and others every three years, depending on the council.

## Licensing checklist

1. Register the business with the URSB (UGX 100,000 to 500,000) and keep the registration certificate and the URSB extract.
2. Get a TIN. It is free.
3. Check that every doctor holds a current UMDPC practising licence and that every nurse and midwife is registered with the UNMC. Allied health staff register with their own council.
4. Apply to KCCA in Kampala, or to your municipal council elsewhere, for the local permit (UGX 50,000 to 200,000).
5. Find out the waste disposal rules that apply to you and arrange environmental clearance for medical waste, and register the clinic with the Personal Data Protection Office, which keeps the register of everyone collecting or processing personal data under sections 5 and 29 of the Data Protection and Privacy Act, 2019 (see [telemedicine in Uganda](/healthcare-in-uganda/)).
6. Agree the inspection date with the UMDPC or UNMC and the local authority, and allow one to two weeks to prepare.
7. Run a mock inspection and fix what you find, starting with backup power and sanitation.
8. Resolve anything the inspectors raise within one to two weeks.
9. Put the pack together: the business registration certificate, the URSB extract, the practising licences and the diploma transcripts.
10. File the pack with the councils and the NDA (UGX 500,000 to 1.5 million in total).
11. Hold the clinic's licence (from the UMDPC for a doctor-run clinic) and the NDA approval for drug storage before the first patient.
12. Put every renewal date in the diary. Some licences renew every year and others every three years.

## Running the clinic once you are licensed

The licence gets you open. Keeping the clinic open is daily routine:

- Move each patient from check-in through the consultation to the pharmacy and the bill, and update the record at every step.
- Count drugs and supplies every week and order ahead, so a late delivery does not leave a shelf empty.
- Give appointments, so patients wait less.
- Meet the staff for a few minutes each day to go over the day's numbers and what went wrong.

Build a few relationships early. Hospitals take your referrals, and VHTs and local doctors can send patients your way because the community already trusts them. Grants from NGOs and PPPs with government bodies can pay for equipment or an expansion, so apply before you need the money.

To find patients, put up a logo and signage (UGX 200,000 to 400,000) and a website (UGX 400,000 to 1.2 million). Offer a discount for referrals to get word of mouth going. Train the staff in how they treat patients at the desk, and open with a day of free health checks and a community health fair to get the first patients through the door.

## Problems to plan for

The same problems catch new clinics out. Plan for each before you open.

1. Paperwork. Regulatory compliance takes more time than most clinicians expect, and it carries on after the licence arrives.
2. Power and water. A generator or solar panels and a reliable water supply cost less than turning patients away.
3. Supply delays. Order drugs well ahead, and count stock every week.
4. Staff shortages. Qualified nurses and midwives are hard to find, so look after the ones you hire.
5. Patients who cannot pay. Post your prices and accept insurance where you can.
6. Funding. Plan the budget carefully and look at grants or loans for the start-up costs.

## Choosing software before opening day

Decide how you will keep records before opening day, because the bills and the patient files have to live somewhere from the first day. Paper works until the receipt book and the stock book stop agreeing. If you are going to use software, choose it before the first patient. Moving paper records across later is slow work that nobody at the desk has time for.
