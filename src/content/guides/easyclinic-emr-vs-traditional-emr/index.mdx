A clinic on paper can run well for years, as long as the right person is at the desk and nobody needs last year's file in a hurry. This guide follows one ordinary day, from the desk to the follow-up call, and says what an EMR changes at each step and where paper still does better.

## Paper and an EMR, side by side

| Moment in the day | On paper | On an EMR such as EasyClinic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A returning patient arrives | Someone searches the register or the file shelf by name and year | The desk types the name and the patient's record opens |
| The doctor needs the last visit | The file reaches the consulting room, if it was found | Once a visit is on the EMR, its notes and medicines are on screen at the next one |
| The prescription | Handwritten, and copied out again at every visit for a patient on regular medicines | Written from shortcuts set up for the way you prescribe, with the last visit's medicines on the same record |
| The bill | Written in the receipt book and added up at closing time | Raised from the visit, with each payment recorded against it |
| "Come back in two weeks" | A line in the file, or the doctor's memory | Booked as an appointment before the patient leaves, with an SMS or email reminder |
| The owner's end-of-day question | Answered by counting the register and the receipt book | Answered from the day's reports |
| The power or the internet goes down | Paper keeps working | EasyClinic needs the connection back, because it does not run fully offline today |

## What changes at the front desk

On paper, the front desk runs on one person's memory. Your receptionist knows which register covers last March and which shelf holds the files for surnames starting with S. On the day they are on leave, the queue slows and the doctor waits for files.

In an EMR, a returning patient is found by name, and a new patient is registered once. The receptionist books the appointment or adds the walk-in to the day's list, and the doctor's list for the day is on the screen without anyone carrying a file down the corridor.

In EasyClinic the receptionist gets a login that is included in the price. It covers registering patients, booking appointments, raising bills and taking payments, and it cannot open the EMR or issue prescriptions, so the medical notes stay with the clinical staff.

Somebody still has to ask the patient for their details and type them in correctly. A misspelt name on paper is a file nobody finds, and a misspelt name in software can become a second record for the same patient. The first days are when the desk sets that habit.

## What changes in the consulting room

For the doctor, the prescription is the part of the day that changes most. A handwritten prescription is flexible: you can draw on it and correct it as you go, and nobody had to train you to do it. Typing it can be slower for the first few patients, and doctors are right to ask about that before they agree to move.

Two things make up for it. Once a patient has been seen on the EMR, their history is on the screen when they sit down, with the last visit's notes and the medicines they are on, so the first minutes of the consult are not spent rebuilding it from a plastic bag of old reports. And the prescription is legible. A patient on four regular medicines no longer has the list copied out by hand at every visit, and the pharmacist no longer calls to ask what a word says.

In EasyClinic the prescription is written from shortcuts set up for the way you prescribe, and the screen warns you when two medicines on it interact. Our promise to doctors who write on paper is that if you give us about two hours, or ten patient records, your prescription writing gets faster. In Kenya, the same prescription can be issued in English and Swahili.

The doctor still decides. Nothing in the record makes the diagnosis for you, and a typed prescription with the wrong dose is as wrong as a handwritten one.

## What changes at billing

On paper, the bill is a receipt book and the day ends with a sum. The consultation fee goes in one line and the dressing or the injection in another. At closing time someone adds up the book and counts the cash drawer. When the two disagree by a few hundred rupees, nobody can say which patient it was.

In EasyClinic the bill is raised from the visit and each payment is recorded against it, so the day-end total comes from the same system as the bills. A service added during the consult reaches the bill without depending on someone remembering to write it down. For the owner, the day's takings are on a dashboard to check before locking up, instead of in a receipt book to add up.

Cash is still counted by a person, and a bill raised for the wrong amount is still wrong. The difference is that every bill and every payment carries a patient's name, so an unpaid bill can be traced to the visit it came from.

## What changes in follow-up

"Come back in two weeks" is where paper loses patients without anyone noticing. On paper, that instruction lives in the doctor's head or on a line in the file, and it depends on the patient remembering. Some do. Others turn up a month later or not at all, and nobody at the clinic can see who did not come back.

In an EMR the follow-up is booked as an appointment before the patient leaves the desk, and it sits on the calendar for that day. EasyClinic sends SMS and email reminders on every plan, and WhatsApp reminders on the Premium plan. Because the visit and the appointment are on the same record, the desk can see from the day's list who did not come, and call them.

Somebody still has to make that call. A reminder helps a patient who forgot, and it does nothing for one who decided not to come.

## What paper still does better

Paper has real advantages, and a clinic moving off it should know what it gives up.

- Paper works when the power or the internet is down. EasyClinic is cloud software and does not run fully offline today, so a clinic whose line drops often needs a backup connection, such as mobile data on a spare phone, and a paper register at the desk for the time it is out.
- Paper needs no training. A new receptionist can use a register on the first morning. Software takes the four training sessions and a few slower days.
- Paper is quick to draw on. A sketch of a wound or a joint takes seconds with a pen, and the same freedom on a screen takes a little practice. A drawn page can still be scanned onto the record.
- Paper costs almost nothing each month. Software is a monthly cost per doctor, which the pricing page sets out plan by plan.

## What happens to the paper files you already have

You do not have to type up the old files before you go live. A simple way is to start each patient's EMR record at their next visit. The desk registers them, and the old file comes off the shelf while the doctor writes today's visit on screen. A patient who never comes back never needs typing in.

Where an old page matters, such as a discharge summary or last year's blood report, scan it and attach it to the patient's record, where it sits beside the visits written in EasyClinic. Keep the paper files themselves for as long as the rules in your country require medical records to be kept.

## How long the move takes

A clinic can be live on EasyClinic in as little as 3 days after the demo. That is the quickest case, and yours may take longer. Training is personalised for you and your clinic staff and split into four short sessions on four days, of 40, 40, 20 and 20 minutes, at times that suit the clinic. That is about two hours in all, and support carries on after day 4, by phone or in writing, at no extra cost on any plan. The [switch plan](/switch/) sets out each session and the steps around it.

Going live and going paperless are different dates. Expect the first days to feel slower than paper, and expect the file shelf to stay in use while returning patients come back one by one.

## If your clinic already has an EMR

Some clinics reading this are not on paper at all. They have an EMR for the records, and some still run the bills or the follow-ups on paper or in a spreadsheet beside it. For those clinics the move has two halves: the records already typed into the old system, and the habits the staff have built around its screens.
