This guide follows the order the work comes in: the area and the kind of clinic first, the paperwork last. The licensing steps are gathered into one numbered checklist near the end, beside the costs in naira. None of the naira figures carries a date, so get a current quote from each office and supplier before you budget.

## Why open a clinic in Nigeria

Nigeria has more patients than its doctors can see. World Health Organisation figures from 2023 put the country at 0.4 doctors for every 1,000 people, in a population of more than 200 million that grew by about 2.1% in 2022, according to [World Bank figures](https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/SP.POP.GROW?locations=NG). Public facilities are underfunded, and private clinics already carry much of the load: Nigeria Health Watch reports that they provide 70% of the care in the cities.

Patients who can pay a fair fee choose a clinic near home that does not keep them waiting all morning. More of them now want preventive care and follow-ups as well as treatment when something goes wrong. Rural areas need maternal care, and chronic disease is rising in the cities. Pick one of those needs and build the clinic around it.

## Start small, with the services patients come back for

You do not need hospital money to open a clinic in Nigeria. Many clinics that are doing well started with two or three services in steady demand and added more as patient numbers grew. Good first services are outpatient consultations, antenatal care, basic diagnostics and follow-ups for chronic disease, because patients return for them.

A small clinic can run well from:

- one consultation room
- a compact waiting area
- a basic treatment room
- records kept in software rather than paper files

Keep costs down without cutting corners on care. Buy refurbished equipment where that is safe and lease the diagnostic machines. Rent in a semi-commercial neighbourhood rather than a premium one, and put in a solar backup system to cut what you spend on generator fuel.

Once consultations are steady, add income that uses the same rooms and staff: point-of-care tests, vaccination packages, health screenings for local companies and paid follow-up plans for chronic patients. Clinics run this way often break even within 6 to 12 months, even with modest patient numbers, although nothing guarantees it.

## Research the area before you sign a lease

Find out how people in the area get care now. The gap between public and private care is wide: Trade.gov reported in 2023 that 60% of public facilities use second-hand equipment because new equipment costs too much. Patients also do not always use the facility nearest them. A 2019 study in BMC Health Services found that 60 to 90% of patients bypass their primary health centre.

Answer four questions about the area:

- What do patients go without? In the rural North it is often antenatal care, because facilities are scarce. In cities such as Lagos it is chronic disease management.
- Where do they go instead, and why?
- Which clinics already serve them, and what do those clinics not offer?
- Is the area safe, and can patients reach it easily?

Run a small survey of your own, and visit each possible site several times to see the demand and the competition for yourself.

## Decide what kind of clinic you are opening

The type of clinic decides who you hire and what you buy, so settle it before anything else.

- General practice, for the widest range of patients.
- Paediatrics, for families with children.
- Diagnostics, where demand in the cities is growing.
- A mobile clinic, to reach remote villages.

Match the clinic to your training, or partner with a specialist for what you cannot cover. Start small so you can test the area before you commit more money; the cost tables further down show what a small clinic takes. Public-private partnerships (PPPs) can help later, when patient numbers justify an expansion.

## Qualifications, and who can own a clinic

Every doctor in the clinic needs an MBBS with the internship completed, and registration with the Medical and Dental Council of Nigeria (MDCN). It takes years, and there is no shortcut. The MDCN licence is renewed every year, so check the current fee with the MDCN, and keeping it means ongoing training (CPD credits) every year, so keep a record of your courses as you go.

Extra skills pay off. A clinician who can do ultrasound is valuable in rural areas where diagnostics are scarce. Nurses and technicians need valid licences of their own, so check them before you hire and keep training the team afterwards.

You do not have to be a doctor to own a clinic in Nigeria. A non-doctor owner must employ a licensed medical doctor to oversee the clinical side, and runs the business side themselves.

## The regulators you will deal with

- The Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC) registers the business, through its [company registration portal](https://icrp.cac.gov.ng/).
- The MDCN registers and licenses doctors.
- The clinic needs its own licence. In Lagos it comes from the [Health Facilities Monitoring and Accreditation Agency (HEFAMAA)](https://hefamaa.lagosstate.gov.ng/), which accredits, inspects, monitors and licenses health facilities, private and public. Outside Lagos, state boards set and enforce their own rules, so ask your state's health authority which office licenses clinics.
- The local government issues the business permit.
- The [National Environmental Standards and Regulations Enforcement Agency (NESREA)](https://nesrea.gov.ng/) enforces environmental laws and standards, including the rules on waste.
- The Nigeria Data Protection Commission (NDPC) enforces the Nigeria Data Protection Act, 2023, and its [registration notice](https://ndpc.gov.ng/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Updated-Guidance-Notice-on-Registtration-2024.pdf) names the health sector among those whose data controllers register with it.
- [NAFDAC](https://www.nafdac.gov.ng/about-nafdac/) regulates the drugs and medical devices themselves, from import and manufacture to distribution and sale. Its list of functions says nothing about licensing a clinic, so if you will stock and dispense drugs, ask your state's health authority what the clinic's pharmacy needs before you buy the first stock.

The clinic also has to follow the rules on waste disposal and on patient data privacy. They are enforced, and a clinic that ignores them risks a fine or closure, so ask each office what applies to you before you sign a lease.

## Write a business plan

A business plan makes you answer the questions a bank or a partner will ask anyway. Keep it to four parts:

- Target market: city or rural patients, and the services you will offer them.
- Money: a start-up budget of ₦500,000 to ₦5 million by this guide's figures, covering the equipment and the first rent and salaries, with a projection of the running costs after that.
- Marketing: signage and a website, both costed in the table below.
- Premises: the rooms and equipment you need on the first day, with room to grow.

Aim to break even within 6 to 12 months. Once you open, compare the plan with the real cash every month, and set your fees at what patients in the area can pay.

## Choose the premises

Location decides who can reach you, and you cannot change it after you sign. Check these first:

- Safety. A secure area that patients and staff can reach easily.
- Patients. Match the area to the people you want to see: families for a paediatric clinic, and a high-population area such as Lagos over a sparsely populated one.
- Visibility. A busy street, with room for a clear sign.
- Power and water. A generator, solar panels or inverters, and a reliable water supply. Outages are frequent in some areas, and a clinic without power cannot see patients.
- Rent. Negotiate in a semi-commercial neighbourhood rather than a premium zone.

Visit each site several times, and count the foot traffic and the clinics already nearby before you decide.

## Services and layout

Start with the services in steady demand:

- Outpatient consultations.
- Basic laboratory tests.
- Maternal health.
- Chronic disease management.
- Vaccinations.

Plan the rooms around patient flow and privacy: a waiting room where patients can sit comfortably, consultation rooms, a treatment room and a pharmacy. Solar power or inverters and a water supply keep the clinic running through outages.

## Equipment and supplies

- Basic equipment, ₦200,000 to ₦1 million: stethoscopes and blood pressure monitors for routine checks, and an autoclave for sterilising.
- Advanced equipment, ₦500,000 to ₦2 million: an ECG for heart monitoring and an ultrasound machine for antenatal scans.
- First stock of medicines and supplies, ₦100,000 to ₦300,000: medicines for common illnesses, and bandages.
- Emergency supplies: first aid kits, kept stocked, and an evacuation plan on the wall.

Leave the advanced machines until patient numbers justify them. Negotiate bulk deals with suppliers, or lease the expensive items to keep the upfront cost down. Test every machine before you pay for it, because a breakdown in the first month stops patient care.

## Hire registered staff

A basic clinic can open with one doctor, one nurse and one person on the front desk. Plan for four or five hires as patient numbers grow.

Check every licence before you make an offer, starting with the MDCN licence for each doctor. Train the team on your clinical protocols and on the records system, and keep training them. Offer bonuses or other incentives, and make the clinic a good place to work, because every nurse who leaves is a gap a small clinic has to fill in a hurry.

## Register, inspect and license the clinic

Most of the time at this stage goes on waiting for other offices, so start early and work in this order.

### Register the business

The CAC registers either a business name or a company, through its [company registration portal](https://icrp.cac.gov.ng/). A business name is the simpler of the two, and a company gives you liability protection. Registration costs ₦10,000 to ₦50,000 and takes one to two weeks if your documents are ready. You will need the ownership details and your business plan.

Then get a tax identification number (TIN). It is free and needs proof of identity. If you are applying for a PPP or your state has rules of its own, a consultant who knows the process can save you time.

### Get the permits

The local government issues the business permit, for ₦20,000 to ₦100,000 depending on the area. The clinic needs its own licence as well: in Lagos, [HEFAMAA](https://hefamaa.lagosstate.gov.ng/) accredits, inspects and licenses health facilities, and elsewhere your state's health authority will tell you which office does. Waste compliance with NESREA's standards costs ₦50,000 to ₦150,000.

### Prepare for the inspection

Book the inspection with the office that licenses the clinic, and expect it one to two weeks after the application. The inspectors check the clinic against health standards, from cleanliness to the fire exits. Switch on every machine yourself before they arrive.

Run a mock inspection of your own first. Walk through the clinic as an inspector would and look for what catches clinics out, such as faulty wiring or a lapse in sanitation. If the inspectors raise concerns, fix them and follow up within days, so the approval does not stall.

### Submit the documents

Put the pack together:

- The CAC registration certificate.
- The MDCN licence for each doctor.
- The waste permit.
- Any document the state or local government office asks for.

Keep the reference number for every application and use it to chase progress. If the paperwork is tied to a PPP, a lawyer who knows the process is worth the fee. Keep everything in one file, because you will need it again for every renewal and audit.

## Licensing checklist

1. Choose a business name or a company, and register it with the CAC through its [company registration portal](https://icrp.cac.gov.ng/) (₦10,000 to ₦50,000). Keep the registration certificate.
2. Get a TIN. It is free and needs proof of identity.
3. Check that every doctor holds a current MDCN licence, and that every nurse and technician holds a valid licence.
4. Apply to the local government for the business permit (₦20,000 to ₦100,000).
5. Apply for the clinic's own licence: in Lagos to [HEFAMAA](https://hefamaa.lagosstate.gov.ng/), elsewhere to the office your state's health authority names.
6. Register the clinic with the [NDPC](https://ndpc.gov.ng/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Updated-Guidance-Notice-on-Registtration-2024.pdf), find out the waste disposal rules that apply to you, and budget ₦50,000 to ₦150,000 for waste compliance with [NESREA](https://nesrea.gov.ng/)'s standards.
7. Book the inspection, and allow one to two weeks after the application to prepare.
8. Run a mock inspection and fix what you find, starting with the wiring and sanitation.
9. Resolve anything the inspectors raise and follow up within days.
10. Submit the pack: the CAC certificate, the MDCN licences, the waste permit and anything the state or local government asks for. Keep every reference number.
11. Keep all the records in one file, ready for renewals and audits.
12. Put every renewal date in the diary, starting with the MDCN licence each year, and keep your CPD credits up to date.

## Running the clinic once you are licensed

The licence gets you open. Keeping the clinic open is daily routine:

- Move each patient from check-in through the consultation to the bill, and update the record at every step.
- Count drugs and supplies every week and order ahead, so a late delivery does not leave a shelf empty.
- Give appointments, so patients wait less.
- Spend a few minutes with the staff at the end of each day on what went well and what did not.

Patients come back to a clinic they trust. Post your prices, keep the rooms clean, put your staff's qualifications where patients can see them, and keep in touch with patients between visits. A clinic that families trust becomes their first choice, rather than the place they go in an emergency.

Build a few relationships early. Hospitals take your referrals and labs run the tests you cannot. Local doctors send patients to clinics they know, so introduce yourself. Grants and PPPs can pay for equipment or an expansion, so apply before you need the money. Health fairs and community events bring in patients from rural areas and build trust with them.

To find patients, put up a logo and signage (₦50,000 to ₦100,000) and build a website (₦100,000 to ₦300,000). A Facebook page reaches a wider audience than the sign. Offer a discount for referrals and ask happy patients for reviews, so word of mouth gets going. Train the staff in how they treat patients at the desk, and open with a day of free check-ups to get the first patients through the door.

## Where new clinics go wrong

The same four mistakes come up again and again.

1. Underestimating the running costs. Budget for the months before patient numbers build, and not only for the opening.
2. Ignoring compliance. The licences need renewing and the rules keep applying after the inspection.
3. Choosing the wrong location. A good clinic on a street patients do not use still struggles.
4. Planning no systems. Manual processes lead to billing mistakes and lost files, and they get worse as the clinic grows.

## Choosing software before opening day

Decide how you will keep records before opening day, because the patient files and the bills have to live somewhere from the first patient. Paper works until the receipt book and the stock count stop agreeing. If you are going to use software, choose it before the first patient, so nobody at the front desk has to copy paper records across later. Choose a system that will still fit when you add a second doctor or a second location.
