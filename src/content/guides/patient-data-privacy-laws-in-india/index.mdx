Patient data law in India is spread across several acts and rules, and it is changing while clinics keep working. This guide explains what each one says about patient records and what that means at the front desk and in the consulting room. It explains the law in general and is not legal advice, so check anything you act on with a lawyer who knows your state.

The biggest change is the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, passed in 2023. Its Rules were notified in November 2025 and give organisations [18 months to comply in phases](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2190014), so the duties arrive in stages. The practical steps are gathered into one numbered checklist near the end.

## Why patient data privacy matters to a clinic

Patients tell a doctor things they tell nobody else, and they do it because they expect it to stay in the room. A patient who thinks the receptionist or the neighbours will hear about it leaves things out, and a doctor working from half a history makes worse decisions. Some patients stop coming at all.

Medical records hold the most sensitive facts about a person, from the diagnosis to the treatment history. Much of it now sits on computers and phones. The cyberattack on AIIMS Delhi showed that a large hospital can be hit, and a small clinic has fewer people watching its systems.

Privacy also makes the care better. A patient who trusts the clinic gives the full history, and the doctor gets the details needed for an accurate diagnosis.

## The laws that cover patient data in India

Several laws overlap on patient data. Here is what each one covers.

### The DPDP Act 2023

The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 is India's data protection law, and it covers the personal data a clinic holds, including health information. The government notified the Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025 in November 2025, and the Rules give organisations an [18-month phased timeline to comply](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2190014). Most of the duties in the Rules that touch a clinic, such as the notice to patients, security safeguards, breach reporting and answering patients' requests, start at the end of that period ([rule 1 of the DPDP Rules](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/11/53450e6e5dc0bfa85ebd78686cadad39.pdf)).

The Act's own duties follow the same timetable. Under the government's [commencement notification, G.S.R. 843(E)](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/11/c56ceae6c383460ca69577428d36828b.pdf), the sections that set a clinic's duties and patients' rights (sections 3 to 17) and the Board's powers to hear complaints and impose penalties (sections 27 to 34) come into force in May 2027, apart from two clauses on consent managers, sections 6(9) and 27(1)(d), which start a year after the notification.

What the Act will mean for a clinic from May 2027:

- It will apply to personal data held in digital form, including data first written on paper and later typed or scanned in ([section 3 of the Act](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2024/06/2bf1f0e9f04e6fb4f8fef35e82c42aa5.pdf)). Records kept only on paper will fall outside the Act, though a doctor's duty of confidentiality still applies to them.
- Patients will get rights over their data: to know what the clinic holds, to have it corrected, to withdraw consent, and to complain if the clinic does not respond.
- The clinic will be able to use data with the patient's consent or for a short list of legitimate uses in [section 7 of the Act](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2024/06/2bf1f0e9f04e6fb4f8fef35e82c42aa5.pdf), one of which is responding to a medical emergency.
- If data leaks, the Act will require the clinic to tell the affected patients and the Data Protection Board of India ([section 8(6) of the Act](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2024/06/2bf1f0e9f04e6fb4f8fef35e82c42aa5.pdf)).
- The Data Protection Board of India will be able to hear complaints and impose financial penalties on organisations that break the Act ([sections 27 and 33 of the Act](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2024/06/2bf1f0e9f04e6fb4f8fef35e82c42aa5.pdf)).

### The IT Act and the SPDI Rules

The older set of rules is the Information Technology (Reasonable Security Practices and Procedures and Sensitive Personal Data or Information) Rules, 2011, usually called the SPDI Rules, made under the Information Technology Act, 2000. They treat medical records as sensitive personal data and ask for the patient's consent before such data is collected. They also expect the data to be used only for the purpose it was collected for, and no more of it to be collected than that purpose needs.

The DPDP Act amends the IT Act ([section 44 of the DPDP Act](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2024/06/2bf1f0e9f04e6fb4f8fef35e82c42aa5.pdf)). Section 44(2), which omits section 43A of the IT Act, comes into force in May 2027 under the same notification, so section 43A and the SPDI Rules made under it remain in place until then. Ask a lawyer how this affects your clinic.

### Medical ethics and the NMC

The National Medical Commission Act, 2019 does not contain a confidentiality rule itself. It gives the [National Medical Commission](https://www.nmc.org.in/) the job of laying down codes of professional ethics, and its Ethics and Medical Registration Board regulates professional conduct, working through the state medical councils where they hold disciplinary powers ([sections 10 and 27 of the NMC Act](https://nmc.org.in/210357_NMC_Act-1-1.pdf)). The duty of confidentiality is in the ethics regulations, covered in the section on doctor-patient confidentiality below.

### Where the Clinical Establishments Act applies

The Clinical Establishments (Registration and Regulation) Act, 2010 applies in the states and union territories that have adopted it, and the [Act's own website](https://clinicalestablishments.mohfw.gov.in/) lists them. The [charter of patients' rights](https://clinicalestablishments.mohfw.gov.in/sites/default/files/2023-06/3181.pdf) approved by its National Council for Clinical Establishments includes a right to confidentiality and privacy during treatment, and a right to a copy of case papers and patient records.

### Laws for specific health concerns

Laws on mental health care and on the termination of pregnancy put extra privacy duties on the clinics that provide those services. If your clinic offers either, read the law that applies before you set up the records for it.

## Doctor-patient confidentiality

Doctor-patient confidentiality means the doctor keeps private what the patient shares. The [Indian Medical Council (Professional Conduct, Etiquette and Ethics) Regulations, 2002](https://www.nmc.org.in/page/rules-regulations-rules-regulations-of-erstwhile-mci-code-of-medical-ethics-regulations-2002) forbid a doctor to disclose a patient's secrets learnt in practice (regulation 7.14), and regulation 2.2 says confidences should never be revealed unless the law requires it. A doctor who breaks the rule can face disciplinary action by the state medical council.

Regulation 7.14 lists three exceptions:

- In a court of law, under orders of the presiding judge.
- Where there is a serious and identified risk to a specific person or community.
- Notifiable diseases, which the regulation says should be reported to the public health authorities at once.

Patients do not separate the doctor from the staff. A receptionist who reads a diagnosis out at the counter loses the clinic the same trust as a doctor who gossips about a case.

Privacy and confidentiality overlap, but they are different things. Privacy is about who can get at a patient's record at all. Confidentiality is about what the doctor, who has seen the record, may pass on.

## Digital health: ABDM and telemedicine

Electronic medical records and remote consultations mean more patient data moves through software, and two national frameworks set rules for it.

The [Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission](https://abdm.gov.in/) (ABDM) is building India's digital health infrastructure, including registries of healthcare providers. Participants in ABDM must follow its Health Data Management Policy, which requires consent before sensitive health data is processed.

The Telemedicine Practice Guidelines, issued in March 2020, set the standards for remote consultations in India, including how patient data is kept secure during them. A consultation by video is still a consultation, and the patient's data needs the same care as it would in the room.

## What the law asks of a clinic

The laws above come down to a handful of habits.

### Consent and a clear explanation

Ask before you collect or share patient data, and tell the patient in plain words how the data will be used. The registration form is the natural place to do it, since every patient fills one in.

### Security

Keep records on systems with passwords and access controls, so each member of staff sees what their job needs and nothing more. Encrypt stored data, and keep backups somewhere secure.

### Patient rights

Patients can ask to see their data and have it corrected. They can also withdraw their consent. Give them a way to ask, and a way to complain if they think their privacy has been breached, and decide who at the clinic handles those requests.

### Training

Train staff on privacy when they join and again regularly. Training is what stops the accidental leak, such as a report sent to the wrong number or a screen left open at the front desk.

### Audits

Check your processes regularly, and watch the systems so problems show up early rather than after a patient complains.

### A plan for a breach

Write down what happens after a cyberattack or a leak before one happens, including who tells the patients and who contacts the Data Protection Board. Under [rule 7 of the DPDP Rules](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/11/53450e6e5dc0bfa85ebd78686cadad39.pdf), once it is in force, a clinic that suffers a personal data breach must tell the affected patients and the Board without delay. Quick action limits the damage.

## Privacy in the clinic day to day

Privacy rules shape the clinic's day from the first phone call to the day a record is finally deleted.

At the front desk, the registration form explains how the patient's personal and medical data will be used, and the patient gives consent there. Under the SPDI Rules, consent comes before health data is collected.

In the consulting room, privacy includes what is said out loud. Talking about a patient in the waiting area or a shared corridor can break confidentiality as surely as a lost file. After the consultation, bills and lab reports need the same care as the clinical notes.

For a clinic that keeps records in software, that means:

- Limiting staff access by role.
- Logging every time a record is opened or changed.
- Securing backups and cloud storage.
- Never sharing patient records casually over unsecured channels.

## What happens when a clinic ignores the rules

The legal risk comes from two directions. A doctor who breaks confidentiality can face disciplinary action from the state medical council, and from May 2027 the DPDP Act will allow the Data Protection Board of India to impose financial penalties once it finds a significant breach of the Act ([section 33](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2024/06/2bf1f0e9f04e6fb4f8fef35e82c42aa5.pdf)).

The damage to the clinic's name can cost more than any penalty. Patients know more about their privacy rights than they did, and one breach can end their trust in a clinic. The clinic loses patients who do not return, and it can lose its partnerships with insurers or hospitals.

Investigations and audits also take time the clinic would rather spend on patients. And the people hurt first are the patients themselves, who face identity theft or discrimination when their records leak.

## The rules are still being phased in

The DPDP Rules [phase in over the 18 months after November 2025](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2190014), so what a clinic is expected to do will grow over that period. At the same time, patients are asking more about how their data is stored and shared.

Clinics that build privacy into their routine now will have less to change later. The checklist below is a start.

## Privacy checklist for your clinic

1. Find out which data protection rules apply to your clinic: the DPDP Act and its Rules as they phase in ([the government's announcement](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2190014)), and the SPDI Rules under the IT Act. Ask a lawyer which apply on the date you read this.
2. Check whether your state or union territory has adopted the [Clinical Establishments Act](https://clinicalestablishments.mohfw.gov.in/), and read the [charter of patients' rights](https://clinicalestablishments.mohfw.gov.in/sites/default/files/2023-06/3181.pdf) approved by its National Council for Clinical Establishments.
3. Add a plain-language explanation of how you use patient data to the registration form, and ask for consent before you collect or share data.
4. Set staff access by role, so each person sees only the records their job needs.
5. Encrypt stored data and keep backups somewhere secure.
6. Log every time a record is opened or changed.
7. Stop sharing patient records over unsecured channels, and keep conversations about patients out of shared spaces.
8. Give patients a way to see and correct their data or withdraw consent. Give them a way to complain too, and name who at the clinic handles both.
9. Make sure every doctor knows the confidentiality duty in the 2002 ethics regulations and its exceptions. The [National Medical Commission](https://www.nmc.org.in/) is the national regulator.
10. Train staff on privacy when they join and again regularly.
11. Audit your processes regularly.
12. Write a breach plan that says who tells the affected patients and who contacts the Data Protection Board of India.
13. Keep patient data only as long as the medical and legal purposes need it, and check the record-keeping periods with a lawyer before deleting anything.
14. If you join [ABDM](https://abdm.gov.in/), follow its Health Data Management Policy.
15. If you consult remotely, follow the Telemedicine Practice Guidelines.

Software cannot make a clinic compliant, because the duties sit with the clinic, and from May 2027 [section 8(1) of the DPDP Act](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2024/06/2bf1f0e9f04e6fb4f8fef35e82c42aa5.pdf) will make the clinic responsible for data a vendor handles on its behalf too. - Where is our patient data hosted, and in which country?
- Who at your company can see our patient records, and in what circumstances?
- Can we set access by role, so the receptionist does not see the clinical notes?
- Does the system log who opened or changed each record?
- What happens to our data when we leave? Can we export all of it, and what happens to your copy afterwards?
