This guide follows the order the work comes in. It starts with the area and the kind of clinic and ends with the staff and the paperwork. The licensing steps are collected into one numbered checklist further down, next to the costs in Ethiopian birr. None of the fees carries a date, so check each one with the office that charges it before you budget.

## Why open a clinic in Ethiopia

Public health facilities in Ethiopia are overcrowded. Patients who can afford it increasingly pay for a shorter wait and a doctor who is there when the clinic says it is open. Private clinics in Addis Ababa, and in cities such as Dire Dawa and Hawassa, see steady patient numbers for that reason.

The government's Health Sector Transformation Plan for 2021 to 2025, known as HSTP II, encouraged private investment and partnerships between public and private providers to close the gaps. The gaps differ by place, and the market survey below is how you find the one near you.

You do not need a hospital to meet that demand. A small clinic can open on ETB 500,000 to 1.5 million and add services as patients come back. Finding the money and getting through the licensing are the hard parts, and both are covered below.

## Start with a market survey

Before you sign a lease, learn how care is organised where you plan to open. The public system starts with health posts, which give basic care in the community, and sends harder cases up to health centres and hospitals. Private clinics are gaining ground as the public facilities fill up, and your competition is whichever of these your patients use now.

Find out four things about the area:

- What patients go without. In rural Oromia it is often maternal care, because facilities are scarce. In Addis Ababa, diabetes and hypertension are rising.
- Where they go now, and how long they wait there.
- Who already serves them, and what those clinics do not offer.
- What it will cost you to serve them. The tables further down list fees, rent, equipment and salaries.

Run a small survey of your own to find the underserved streets and what patients there prefer. Visit each possible site more than once, on different days, before you decide.

## Decide what kind of clinic you are opening

The type of clinic decides who you hire and what you buy, so settle it first. Four kinds work in Ethiopia, depending on local demand:

- General practice, for the widest range of patients.
- Maternal health, where rural demand is high.
- Diagnostics, where the towns are growing.
- A mobile clinic, to reach remote areas with portable equipment.

Match the clinic to your training, or partner with a specialist for what you cannot do yourself. In rural Amhara, antenatal care is the gap because midwives are overstretched. In Addis Ababa, diagnostics are in demand as urban diseases rise.

Start small. A small clinic costs ETB 500,000 to 1.5 million to set up, which lets you test the market before you commit to a bigger building. Public-private partnerships (PPPs) can help later, with funding or infrastructure when you want to expand.

## Write a business plan

A business plan makes you answer the questions a bank or a partner will ask anyway. Keep it to four parts:

- Market: the need you are meeting, such as maternal care in a rural area, and the niche that sets you apart, such as diagnostics.
- Patients: the urban middle class, or an underserved rural area. Pick one and shape the services around it.
- Services: consultations and laboratory tests to start, with vaccinations if the area needs them.
- Money: a start-up budget of ETB 500,000 to 2 million and running costs of about ETB 300,000 a month, set against the patients you expect each month.

Aim to break even within 12 to 18 months. Once you are open, compare the forecast with the real cash flow every month and adjust.

## Find the money

Most clinics are paid for with some mix of these:

- Personal savings for the first capital.
- A bank loan. Ask more than one bank for its current rate and terms before you commit.
- A loan from a local microfinance institution.
- A public-private partnership, which can bring funding or infrastructure support and reduce what you have to find yourself.
- A grant, where you meet the funder's criteria. Expect to be asked for a plan showing what the clinic will do for the community.

Build a budget that covers the equipment and the staff, and track spending every month so you see a shortfall before it arrives. Keep 10 to 20% of the budget aside for surprises, such as an equipment repair.

## Choose the premises

Location decides your patient numbers more than anything else you control. Check these before you sign:

- Access. Paved roads and public transport, so patients can reach you. Avoid the remote highlands, where the journey keeps patients away.
- Power and water. Electricity with a solar backup, and clean water. Supply in rural areas is unreliable, and a clinic that loses power or water cannot keep running.
- Visibility. A busy spot with room for a clear sign, which matters most where other clinics are nearby, as in much of Addis Ababa.
- Rent. Expect ETB 20,000 to 80,000 a month in Addis Ababa, or ETB 5,000 to 20,000 a month in a rural area of the south.
- Patient flow. Visit each site several times, and watch how busy the clinics already there are.

## Hire the team

A new clinic can open with a small team and grow to four to six staff as patient numbers rise.

- A doctor to lead clinical care.
- Nurses, at ETB 15,000 to 30,000 a month.
- A midwife for maternal services, at ETB 10,000 to 20,000 a month.
- A receptionist to manage patients at the desk, at ETB 5,000 to 10,000 a month.
- A secretary for the records, at ETB 6,000 to 12,000 a month.

Every clinician on the team needs a current professional licence. The Ministry of Health regulates health professionals and sets the licensing examination for doctors, nurses, midwives and other health professions, as its [licensing examination page](https://www.moh.gov.et/index.php/ehple) explains. Check every licence before you make an offer.

Doctors are in short supply, and the staff you hire are hard to keep. Offer bonuses or help with housing. Train everyone on your clinical protocols and on the records system when they start, and keep the training going with regular sessions after that.

## Equip the clinic

- Basic equipment, ETB 200,000 to 800,000: stethoscopes and blood pressure monitors for routine checks, and an autoclave for sterilising.
- Diagnostic equipment, ETB 500,000 to 1.5 million: an ultrasound machine for prenatal care and an ECG for heart monitoring.
- Furniture, ETB 100,000 to 300,000: patient beds, and chairs and desks for the waiting area and the consulting rooms.
- Sourcing. Buy from local suppliers, or import through a PPP, and negotiate bulk deals to bring the price down and the delivery forward.
- Maintenance. In a remote area a broken machine can sit idle for a long time, so keep a maintenance schedule and train one member of staff to handle basic repairs.

Leave the ultrasound and the ECG until patient numbers justify them, unless diagnostics is the reason the clinic exists.

## Register and license the clinic

Most of the time at this stage goes on waiting for other offices. Licensing can take 3 to 6 months when the documents and the inspections go through without delays, and longer when a document is missing. Start early.

### Register with the Ministry of Health

Register the clinic with the [Ministry of Health](https://www.moh.gov.et/). You submit the facility plans and the staff credentials, and registration costs ETB 50,000 to 150,000. This registration is the clinic's approval to operate.

### Get the municipal permit

The local authority issues a municipal permit after its own health and safety checks. It costs ETB 20,000 to 100,000, and the fee and the checks vary by region.

### Check the professional licences

Every clinician needs a current professional licence before the first patient. Keep a copy of each one on file, because the Ministry of Health registration asks for the staff credentials.

### Get the waste management permit

Clinical waste needs a waste management permit from the [Environmental Protection Authority](https://epa.gov.et/), at ETB 30,000 to 80,000. Ask which permit applies to a clinic of your size before you apply.

### Keep it all current

Plan for renewals from the start. Put each licence's expiry date in the diary, and keep the records the renewal will ask for, so the health rules can change without catching you out.

## Licensing checklist

1. Put the facility plans together and collect a copy of every staff member's credentials.
2. Check that every clinician holds a current professional licence. The Ministry of Health runs the [licensing examination for health professionals](https://www.moh.gov.et/index.php/ehple).
3. Register the clinic with the [Ministry of Health](https://www.moh.gov.et/) (ETB 50,000 to 150,000), submitting the facility plans and the staff credentials.
4. Apply to the local authority for the municipal permit (ETB 20,000 to 100,000) and pass its health and safety checks.
5. Get the waste management permit from the [Environmental Protection Authority](https://epa.gov.et/) (ETB 30,000 to 80,000).
6. Allow 3 to 6 months for all of it if no document is missing, and follow up with each office until the approvals come through.
7. Hold every approval before the first patient walks in.
8. Put every renewal date in the diary and keep the records each renewal will ask for.

## Billing and insurance

Decide how patients will pay before the first one arrives.

- Insurance. Learn the claim requirements of Community-Based Health Insurance (CBHI) and of any NGO-supported scheme you plan to accept, and file claims promptly.
- Cash. Set a clear policy on fees paid upfront or in instalments, and accept digital payments so patients in town and in the country have more than one way to pay.
- Discounts. A sliding scale for low-income patients keeps the clinic within reach of the people who need it most.
- Records. Record every transaction in the records system, so the bills and the cash agree and any error is easy to trace.

## Talking to patients

Patients come back to a clinic that explains things and follows up.

- Explain conditions such as diabetes clearly, on a leaflet or in person, so patients can manage them at home.
- Book follow-up visits or calls for patients with chronic diseases.
- Offer care in Amharic and in the local languages of your area.
- Ask for feedback with a suggestion box or a short survey, and act on it.
- Take part in national health days to reach people who have not visited yet.

## Finding the first patients

- A website, at ETB 50,000 to 150,000, with your services and a way to book.
- Facebook and Instagram advertising, at ETB 10,000 to 30,000 a month, aimed at the patients you want.
- A logo and signage, at ETB 20,000 to 50,000.
- Referrals from local pharmacies and health posts, which you earn by getting to know the people who run them.
- Health camps or workshops, to bring in the first patients and earn the community's trust.

## How profitable is a clinic in Ethiopia?

A well-planned clinic in Ethiopia can break even within 12 to 18 months, and clinics that combine outpatient care with basic diagnostics typically get there in that time. Nothing guarantees it. The money comes from consultations, diagnostics, maternal care, chronic disease management and insurance reimbursements through CBHI schemes. What gets a clinic to break-even is controlled costs and a steady flow of patients.

## Mistakes to avoid when starting a clinic in Ethiopia

When a new clinic struggles, the cause is often a planning error made before it opened. These are the ones to watch for:

1. Underestimating the licensing time. The Ministry of Health registration and the municipal permit can each take months when the documents are incomplete.
2. Choosing a site without reliable power or water. It shows in the care the clinic can give.
3. Opening services the area does not need. A high-end diagnostic clinic in a low-income rural area can struggle to find patients.
4. Not investing in keeping staff. Clinics that lose their nurses face gaps in service.

## When to expand

Most clinics consider expanding after 12 to 24 months of stable operations. Expand too early and the finances strain, but wait too long and the growth goes to someone else. The signs to watch for are patients waiting every day and revenue that rises month after month. More referrals from nearby health posts and pharmacies point the same way.

There are four ways to grow:

- Add diagnostic services.
- Extend the opening hours.
- Hire specialists.
- Open a second branch in another district.

## Choosing software before opening day

Decide how you will keep records before opening day, because the bills and the patient files have to live somewhere from the first patient. Electronic records keep each patient's history in one place and make prescription errors easier to catch. Digital billing makes claims to CBHI or an NGO-supported programme easier to trace. If you are going to use software, choose it before the first patient. Moving paper records across later is slow work that nobody at the desk has time for.
